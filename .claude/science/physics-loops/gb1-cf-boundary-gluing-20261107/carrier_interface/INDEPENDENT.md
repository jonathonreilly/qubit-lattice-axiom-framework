# Independent carrier-interface check

Status: bounded independent pure-proof check complete and frozen on 2026-11-09; this is not an audit verdict or a retained-status claim. Exact provenance and independence limitations are in `CHECKPOINT.md`.

The task supplies the detector and its formation dynamics. The current four axioms supply neither this dynamics nor a Born rule, a preparation family, an observer-access restriction, a Hilbert-space state interpretation, or an unrestricted effect family. ## 1. Interface and mathematical reading

Let `C={0,...,K-1}`, `B={0,...,M-1}`, with `K,M>=2`, and `T>=1` preparation labels. Every integer denotes that scalar times the identity matrix. A legal state contains one detector and one external label, with any permitted translation and proper cubic rotation applied to the whole configuration. Legal prefixes add the prescribed fine record, then the prescribed coarse record. The existing seed and label records are supplied initial data; their creation is not generated by this two-birth process. Simultaneous superpositions of several detectors and arbitrary extra initial records are not included in this domain.

The content kernel is a function of the center-excluded radius-six record neighborhood. A detector pattern gives a deterministic content kernel even if the center is already occupied; occupancy separately sets its formation hazard to zero. This reading is necessary to respect the specified center-excluded kernel. Defining its deterministic-versus-fallback choice by occupancy instead would not be a center-excluded content law.

The native detector-output interface consists of the two formed records' contents and fixed output positions. The mathematical timed history is also examined below as a stronger information channel; interpreting timing as observable is an additional assumption, not native content readout. Fixed headers and program may be included without adding source information. Reading the incoming carrier itself exposes `C`; reading the separate external label exposes the preparation label. The latter observation is outside the carrier-only/detector-output interface and is not forbidden by the Record axiom.

## 2. Local construction checks

Write `S0` for the six detector seed records, `f=3e1`, `z=3e1+e2`, and `L=20e1`. The three legal prefixes are `S0+label`, `S0+f+label`, and `S0+f+z+label`, with the prescribed contents. The following arguments hold for every valid carrier and program and commute with the allowed spatial copies.

### 2.1 Alphabet and fallback law

The program representation is bijective. For `0<=q<Q=M^K 2^M`, the remainder modulo `M^K` has exactly `K` base-`M` digits, giving `g`, and the quotient has exactly `M` binary digits, giving `h`. Leading zero digits are permitted. Conversely the specified digit sum lies in `[0,Q-1]` and gives those digits.

All carrier, program, fine and coarse values lie in the alphabet `A`: `Q>=2^K>=K`, `Q>=M`, and the outputs are in `0,...,M-1` and `{0,1}`. Headers and labels are distinct negative integers, distinct from every data value and from each other. Thus `|A|=Q+4+T`.

For a visible neighborhood with `k` records and `l` records equal to `I`, `0<=l<=k`, hence

`0 < 1/[2(k+1)] <= p=(1/2+l)/(1+k) <= 1-1/[2(k+1)] < 1`.

The fallback measure is normalized and gives every member of `A` mass at least `1/(2|A|)>0`. No positivity is claimed outside `A` or outside the supported value of a deterministic kernel. A finite-support measure on the full possibility domain `M_2(C)` is permitted by the stated support reading of Admissibility; full support on every matrix is not an axiom.

The law is genuinely neighborhood dependent. At a site far from all records, the fallback has `k=l=0`, hence `p=1/2`; at `19e1`, only the external negative label is visible, hence `k=1,l=0,p=1/4`. These different fallback laws occur in legal configurations. The chosen integer scalar matrices are algebraically defined using the identity and integers; they require no matrix basis or privileged lattice direction.

### 2.2 All-site confinement, including infinitely many exterior sites

Any successful parse must see the uniquely typed four negative headers. Their positions determine one anchor and the ordered vectors from header `-1` to headers `-2,-3,-4`. In a legal state these vectors form the single allowed proper cubic frame. There is no alternative anchor or frame: there is one record of each header type, and all other records have different values.

Consequently every successful detector parse in the entire lattice identifies the same two output coordinates `f,z`. An enabled center must equal one of these coordinates. Every other center has rate zero even if it sees a complete header set. A center missing any header cannot parse a different detector. This proves confinement over all of `Z^3`, without a finite-box scan.

Every two detector coordinates are at L1 distance at most five, so either output center sees every required other detector record inside radius six. The closest detector coordinate to `L` is `f`, at distance seventeen; `z` is at distance eighteen. Thus the external label is invisible to either output center at every prefix. Translations and proper cubic rotations preserve L1 distances, header types, the ordered frame, and both output locations, so the same proof covers every spatial copy.

Initially exactly `f` is enabled. At `z`, the prescribed fine record is missing. After `f` forms, it is occupied and only `z` is enabled. After `z` forms, both candidate output sites are occupied and no site is enabled. All exterior fallback content laws remain irrelevant to births because their rates are zero. In particular there is no sequence of exterior births relaying the label toward the detector.

### 2.4 Zero, absence, and termination

A zero scalar is a present record with a content-determined readout. It contributes one to `k` and zero to `l`. An absent site has no record, no readout, and no contribution to `k`. The pattern parser tests record presence and prescribed contents separately. In particular `g(a)=0` still creates the mandatory fine record and enables the coarse step; it is not treated as a missing record. No scalar value is assigned to absence.

Interpret the stated rates as continuous-time formation intensities, with an independent rate-one exponential waiting time on activation. There is one active clock at the first two prefixes, and none thereafter. The two waits `W1,W2` are independent `Exp(1)` variables, independent of the carrier value and preparation label. Exactly two births occur almost surely; total completion time is `W1+W2`, with mean two and distribution `P(W1+W2<=t)=1-(1+t) exp(-t)` for `t>=0`. Termination is almost sure, not a deterministic deadline. The process has no infinite-volume explosion because there are only two births and at most one positive rate at a time.

### 2.5 What boundary has been proved

Conditional on the carrier `a` and fixed program, the detector's timed output law is

`R_a = delta_(g(a),h(g(a))) tensor [Exp(1) tensor Exp(1)]`.

It is the same for every allowed external preparation label. The rule therefore gives a closed causal input boundary for detector histories **on the supplied legal domain**. It does not give immunity to arbitrary environmental interventions. For example, an additional externally supplied record within the output neighborhood could fail the no-extra-records test and suppress a birth; such a state is not legal here. A separate detector inserted elsewhere is also outside the single-detector domain.

Nor does the boundary forbid a reader from inspecting `L`. Its content identifies the label. Excluding that direct observation is an explicit interface choice, while the absence of dynamical influence from `L` to the detector is proved from the actual law. One must also hold apparatus placement/orientation fixed, or quotient by it, across the preparations being compared: a preparation-dependent visible spatial placement could itself be a side channel. Covariance of the family is not an assertion that such placement is unobservable.

Thus the literal-lattice construction passes the local-law test under the center-excluded-kernel reading above. Its scope depends on the supplied preparation domain, rate law, observer interface, and common apparatus setup; these are not derived from the four axioms.

## 3. Finite-carrier information statements

These statements concern complete trials and a fixed apparatus choice or source-independent adaptive policy. The preparation label is `Lambda`; `C` has `K` possible values. Write `mu_lambda(c)=P(C=c|Lambda=lambda)`. The apparatus may have arbitrary internal variables `A`, even correlated with `C`, but must have one common conditional kernel `alpha_d(dA|C)` for all labels. Conditional on `C,A`, the full future process, including clocks, stopping, failures, and any outside inputs, must have source-independent kernels. A policy may use observed history and its own source-independent randomness; a policy secretly supplied with the label is excluded.

### 3.1 Factorization and effects

Integrating the apparatus and future-history kernels gives one channel

`R_{d,policy}(dH|c) = integral K_{d,policy}(dH|c,A) alpha_d(dA|c)`.

All kernel factors are independent of the label. The same remains true when settings are chosen sequentially from the past: products/integrals of the common conditional factors still define a common history channel. Thus

`P_lambda(dH) = sum_c mu_lambda(c) R(dH|c)`,

or `Lambda -> C -> H`. The integral, rather than a sum over histories, accommodates the continuous exponential waiting times. Failure, noncompletion at a deadline, and inconclusive events must remain outcomes of this channel. This argument assumes a well-defined process/history kernel; it does not derive one from the axioms.

For every measurable output event `E`, define

`e_E(c)=R(E|c)`.

Then `0<=e_E(c)<=1` and `P_lambda(E)=sum_c mu_lambda(c)e_E(c)`. This is an affine pairing of carrier probability vectors with effects. For a partition of histories into outcomes `E_y`, the effects are nonnegative and sum to the all-ones function. Complements and disjoint event sums obey the corresponding elementary identities. The actually attainable effects are the ones induced by the supplied channels and allowed operations; this theorem does not make all of `[0,1]^K` physically available.

Any observer given `C` can simulate `R` and then any decision based on `H`. Thus `C` dominates `H` for every decision problem. Additional processing of a fixed history is another channel and cannot improve distinguishability. An adaptive experiment that obtains new carrier-dependent observations may improve on a particular earlier readout, but its entire transcript is still bounded by `C`. Its availability is an additional dynamical assumption, not supplied by the two-birth native detector.

### 3.2 Total variation, sharpness, and equality

For two carrier laws `mu,nu`, put `v_c=mu(c)-nu(c)` and

`delta=TV(mu,nu)=(1/2) sum_c |v_c|=sum_(v_c>0) v_c`.

For any effect `e` in `[0,1]^K`,

`-delta <= sum_c v_c e(c) <= delta`.

The upper inequality follows by replacing `e` with one on positive terms and zero on negative terms; the lower follows by reversing those choices. For `delta>0`, upper equality holds exactly when `e(c)=1` on `v_c>0` and `e(c)=0` on `v_c<0`; values where `v_c=0` do not matter. Lower equality reverses zero and one. Consequently

`TV(mu R,nu R) <= TV(mu,nu)`.

This bound is sharp over available channels, but a restricted detector need not attain it. More precisely, normalize the positive and negative parts of `v` to probability laws `v_plus/delta` and `v_minus/delta`. Then

`TV(mu R,nu R)=delta * TV((v_plus/delta)R,(v_minus/delta)R)`.

Therefore equality for this pair holds exactly when the two last output laws are mutually singular. Equivalently there is an output event which has probability one for every positive-sign carrier and zero for every negative-sign carrier. For a finite output alphabet, this says no outcome receives positive channel mass from both a positive-sign and a negative-sign carrier. The case `delta=0` is trivial equality.

A sharper channel-specific coefficient is

`eta(R)=max_(c,c') TV(R(.|c),R(.|c'))`,

for which `TV(mu R,nu R)<=eta(R) TV(mu,nu)`. To prove it, express the normalized positive and negative output laws as convex mixtures and compare each cross-pair; their mixture distance is at most the largest pair distance. This coefficient is optimal, because a pair of point carrier laws attaining the finite maximum gives equality. For a fixed nonzero `v`, equality requires one common maximizing event `E` satisfying `R(E|c)-R(E|c')=eta(R)` for every `c` in the positive support and `c'` in the negative support, after orienting the event. This condition is also sufficient. When `eta=0`, every pair is collapsed.

In the native detector the exact fine-output distance is

`TV(g_*mu,g_*nu)=(1/2) sum_b |sum_(g(c)=b) v_c|`.

It equals the input distance precisely when each nonempty `g`-fiber contains no strictly positive and strictly negative entries of `v` together. The same statement applies to coarse fibers under `h o g`. Timing is an independent product factor and changes none of these distances.

### 3.3 Guessing, perfect discrimination, and information

For binary priors `pi,1-pi`, optimal unconditional success with the carrier is

`Pguess(C)=sum_c max(pi mu(c),(1-pi)nu(c)) = (1+||pi mu-(1-pi)nu||_1)/2`.

This follows by choosing the larger prior-weighted likelihood at each carrier. The history cannot do better. Equality has exactly the no-cancellation condition above for the signed vector `pi mu-(1-pi)nu`. For equal priors the bound is `(1+TV(mu,nu))/2`.

For a finite preparation family with prior `pi_lambda`,

`Pguess(H)<=Pguess(C)=sum_c max_lambda [pi_lambda mu_lambda(c)] <= min(1,K max_lambda pi_lambda)`.

The first inequality follows by moving a maximum inside the nonnegative sum/integral over carriers. Equality holds exactly when, at almost every observed history, there is a single label maximizing `pi_lambda mu_lambda(c)` for every contributing carrier `c`. One may use densities of the finitely many measures `R(.|c)` relative to their sum to interpret “contributing” for continuous histories. This is the condition that a common carrier-optimal decision survives each output mixture. For a uniform prior on `T` labels, the bound becomes `min(1,K/T)` and is sharp over preparation families and channels: use distinct point carriers for `T<=K`, or map the `T>=K` labels onto all `K` carriers.

Perfect discrimination of a positive-prior preparation family requires disjoint supports of its carrier laws; identical or overlapping carrier laws cannot become perfectly distinguishable downstream. It additionally requires that the history channel keep those supports separated. Conversely these two conditions give perfect discrimination. Thus at most `K` nonempty preparation distributions can be perfectly distinguished through a closed `K`-valued carrier. A full set of `K` such laws must consist of the `K` point laws, up to relabeling. This is a bound on source information crossing the chosen interface, not a count of all global label records.

With logarithms base two, the Markov factorization gives

`I(Lambda;H) <= I(Lambda;C) <= H(C) <= log K`,

and also `I(Lambda;H)<=H(Lambda)<=log T`.

For the first inequality, expand `I(Lambda;C,H)` in two orders. The term `I(Lambda;H|C)` is zero, leaving the difference `I(Lambda;C|H)>=0`. Conditional mutual information is an average relative entropy of a joint posterior against the product of its marginals; nonnegativity and the product-form equality case follow from `-ln x>=1-x`. Equality holds exactly when the preparation posterior given `C` is recoverable from `H`, equivalently `Lambda` and `C` are conditionally independent given `H`. This need not mean that all of `C` is recoverable. Equality in the next bound requires `H(C|Lambda)=0`, and equality in the last requires a uniform carrier, because `log K-H(C)` is relative entropy against the uniform law. Simultaneous saturation at `log K` requires all these conditions. For the channel from freely chosen `C` to `H`, capacity reaches `log K` exactly when its `K` output laws are mutually singular; uniform carrier use then attains it. A fixed restricted preparation menu need not attain any of these upper bounds.

### 3.4 Why weaker independence assumptions fail

1. **Marginal apparatus independence is insufficient.** Let `Lambda` be a fair bit, `C` an independent fair bit, and `A=C xor Lambda`. The marginal of `A` is independent of `Lambda`, as is `C`, but observing `(C,A)` recovers `Lambda`. Here the conditional apparatus law given `C` depends on `Lambda`, violating the required `alpha(A|C)` condition.
2. **Separate source-independent output marginals are insufficient.** Let `C` be constant, let `R` be a fair bit, and output `Y1=R`, `Y2=R xor Lambda`. Each output separately is source independent, while the pair reveals the label. The second conditional kernel given the first history is source dependent. A joint-history condition cannot be replaced by a per-output marginal condition.
3. **Source-independent contents alone are insufficient.** With a constant carrier and constant output content, exponential formation rates chosen as `r_lambda` convey label information in timing whenever the rates differ. Formation rates and clock correlations are part of the required history kernel.
4. **Local detector syntax alone is insufficient without closure.** A readable external label, an incoming external signal, a source-dependent setting or placement, or a source-dependent stop/selection rule can bypass the carrier. A fixed law conditioned on a global state that already contains the label does not by itself establish operational conditional independence from that label.

Each is an explicit counterexample to the weaker statement, not a universal obstruction to an interface model.

### 3.5 Complete trials and postselection

Postselection changes the probability laws being compared. Let `C={0,1,2}`, with equal source priors and

`mu_0=(1-s)delta_0+s delta_1`, `mu_1=(1-s)delta_0+s delta_2`, `0<s<1`.

A copied fine output has unconditional total variation `s` and optimal guessing probability `(1+s)/2`. Keep only outputs in `{1,2}`. The retained trials perfectly identify the source, but occur with probability `s`; the discarded output `0` is a real shared record, not absence. There is no violation of the unconditional bound. If a success flag has the same probability `s` for both sources, the complete-output distance decomposes as `s TV(success laws)+(1-s)TV(failure laws)`, so in particular `s TV(success laws)<=TV(carrier laws)`. With unequal acceptance probabilities, even that simplified expression must be replaced by the weighted subprobability measures. A capacity or guessing claim must not silently discard failures or noncompletion.

## 4. Native geometry, capacity, and quantum comparison

Let `r=|g(C)|<=min(K,M)` and `s=|h(g(C))|<=min(r,2)`. The following distinctions are necessary: the recorded fine/coarse output, direct access to the input carrier, a selected finite preparation menu, and the family of all selectable programs are different interfaces.

### 4.1 One fixed program

If the fine record is observed, the operational state is

`p_lambda(b)=sum_(g(c)=b) mu_lambda(c)`.

The coarse record is a deterministic function of this value and adds no information. The two waiting times are independent ancillary randomness. Carrier distributions with the same `g_*mu` are indistinguishable by this interface. If only the coarse record is observed, replace `g` by `h o g`; the state is then a probability vector on `s` values.

For the specified `T` preparation procedures, the actual state menu is just the `T` points `{p_lambda}`, with possible repetitions. If independent classical preparation mixing is allowed, its state space is `conv{p_lambda}`, of affine dimension at most `min(T-1,r-1)`. A finite menu does not automatically contain the entire simplex. If arbitrary carrier distributions are supplied as controllable preparations, the fixed-program fine state space is the full `Delta_(r-1)`. With a fixed finite menu plus mixing, that whole simplex is available exactly when each used fine value has a menu preparation supported entirely in its `g`-fiber; this requires `T>=r`. Choosing `T` arbitrary preparation laws can realize any chosen `T` points of this simplex.

For static fine-record events, the native effect vectors are

`e_B(c)=1[g(c) in B]`, for subsets `B` of the used fine values.

The actually formed coarse record selects the particular binary effect `1[h(g(c))=1]`; it does not create every possible extra coarse record. Source-independent randomized postprocessing of the fine outcome yields exactly the fiber-constant effects

`e(c)=u(g(c))`, `0<=u(b)<=1`.

If complete continuous timing is declared observable and arbitrary measurable history events are allowed, these same unsharp effects are already history events: choose `W1<=-ln(1-u(b))` conditional on fine value `b`, with the empty/full conventions for `u=0,1`. This uses the independent clock as a randomizer. It creates no additional carrier information and does not follow from content-only Record readout without a timing-access assumption. Static record events alone should not be silently replaced by this larger effect set. Entropies and capacities below use base-two logarithms.

The fine-output channel has capacity `log r` when all carrier input distributions are available: the output entropy is at most `log r`, and selecting one representative carrier per used fine value uniformly attains it. The maximum number of perfectly distinguishable carrier preparations is `r`. The corresponding coarse-only quantities are `log s` and `s`. For a fixed preparation menu the actual source capacity is

`max_pi [H(sum_lambda pi_lambda p_lambda)-sum_lambda pi_lambda H(p_lambda)]`,

which is at most `log min(T,r)` but can be strictly smaller or zero. The bound is sharp over freely chosen menus. Directly reading the incoming carrier instead gives the `K`-valued input interface, with `log K` available only when the necessary preparations are supplied. Reading the external label is a different, larger interface.

### 4.2 All programs within one fixed `K,M` law

Changing the valid program is an apparatus choice. For every subset `S` of `C`, one can choose `g(c)=1[c in S]`, using only fine labels `0,1`, and choose `h(0)=0,h(1)=1`. This is available for every `M>=2` and implements the binary indicator `1_S`. Thus all sharp binary carrier tests are present across the program family. In particular, two distinct carrier distributions are distinguished by some program: a singleton effect detects any differing coordinate.

This establishes the following binary state/effect geometry, with its preparation qualifications:

- When arbitrary carrier laws are available, the state space separated by all programs is `Delta_(K-1)`.
- For a fixed finite preparation menu and independent preparation mixing, it is the polytope `conv{mu_lambda}`, possibly lower dimensional; if two listed laws coincide they are the same operational state.
- Native static binary effects across all programs include exactly all `0/1` vectors on `C`. Independent classical mixtures of those tests give every effect in `[0,1]^K`. One explicit mixture for an effect `e` assigns a subset `S` probability `product_(c in S)e(c) product_(c not in S)(1-e(c))`; its indicator has expectation `e(c)` for every carrier.
- More generally, every stochastic channel `W(b|c)` with `M` fine labels is a mixture of native deterministic `g` maps: give map `g` probability `rho(g)=product_c W(g(c)|c)`. Summing over all maps gives one; summing over the maps with `g(c)=b` gives `W(b|c)`. The random map is selected independently of the actual carrier and source.

These are finite-dimensional classical probability constructions. Optional mixing is an additional allowed operation, not an axiom-derived effect-completeness rule. In particular full binary effects do not establish that every possible multi-outcome measurement is present.

### 4.3 Parameter bounds and common refinements

A single native fine readout has at most `M` values and actually uses at most `r<=K`. A collection of deterministic binary tests has a common native deterministic fine refinement precisely when the number of distinct joint membership patterns on `C` is at most `M`: assign one fine label per pattern. This necessity follows because carriers with different joint patterns must have different fine labels; sufficiency follows by that assignment and subsequent classical interpretation of the fine label.

For example, with `K=3,M=2`, the tests `{0}` and `{1}` are each native binary tests, but their joint patterns distinguish all three carriers, so there is no single native fine refinement for both. Three fine labels suffice, hence `M>=3` is required for that refinement. Identity copying of all carrier symbols requires `M>=K`. It cannot be claimed within an `M=2,K>2` law by changing the output alphabet halfway through the argument. A mathematical latent common refinement by `C` is not automatically an available detector output.

Independent random mixtures of programs do not repair a missing sharp refinement. If the selected program is observed, each positive-probability program branch would itself have to separate every distinct joint pattern; it still has only `M` fine labels. Hiding the program cannot help: even the pair of fine and coarse outputs is a function of the program and fine output. Independent timing likewise cannot turn an unresolved deterministic membership pattern into a perfectly known one.

If a program is selected independently of both source and carrier, and its identity is recorded, then

`I(Lambda;Program,Fine)=sum_q rho(q) I(Lambda;Fine|Program=q) <= log min(K,M)`.

The maximum over such choices is already attained by a suitable fixed program and suitable preparations. Hidden program mixing cannot exceed it either. With a fixed selected menu there is additionally the `log T` bound, and the actual capacity may be smaller. The analogous coarse-only bound is one bit. An observed source-dependent program would itself be a source channel and is excluded. These bounds concern one native detector execution; an additional adaptive multi-query protocol is not contained in the two-birth law.

### 4.4 Aliasing and genuine carrier mediation

The carrier is not merely a renamed preparation label. For every `K>=2`, choose `M>=K`, a copy program `g(c)=c`, and `T=2K` distinct external labels. Set

`mu_(2c)=mu_(2c+1)=delta_c`.

Both labels in each pair give exactly the same detector-history distribution, despite being different physical external records. Different pairs give different copied carrier symbols. Under uniform label priors, the detector carries `log K` bits about the label, while `H(Lambda)=log(2K)` and optimal label guessing is only `1/2`. Direct external-label readout would remove that aliasing, confirming why it is a different interface. The `K` copied carrier states remain perfectly distinguishable; duplicate labels neither create nor remove those states.

Overlap is also preserved nontrivially. For two labels choose

`mu_0=(1-epsilon)delta_0+epsilon delta_1`,

`mu_1=epsilon delta_0+(1-epsilon)delta_1`, `0<epsilon<1/2`.

A copy program gives total variation `1-2epsilon` and equal-prior guessing success `1-epsilon`, not perfect label recovery. Any two labels assigned the same non-point mixture, for example `(delta_0+delta_1)/2`, remain exactly aliased. The laws can be chosen this way because the preparation family is supplied freely; a particular fixed family may instead have other overlaps or omit point preparations entirely.

### 4.5 Density-matrix/POVM comparison

This is a comparison theorem, not the detector's dynamics and not an axiom interpretation. Suppose one tries to represent the **entire carrier-only interface** of a copy program by states `rho_c` on `C^d` and one POVM `{E_b}` satisfying

`Tr(rho_c E_b)=1[b=c]` for every carrier `c`.

Here `M>=K` is required by the native copy program. Each `rho_c` has trace one and nonzero support; `0<=E_b<=I`. From `Tr(rho_c E_c)=1`, positivity implies that `E_c` equals the identity on the support of `rho_c`. From `Tr(rho_c' E_c)=0` for `c'!=c`, it equals zero on the support of `rho_c'`. The eigenspaces of the self-adjoint `E_c` with eigenvalues one and zero are orthogonal. Therefore the `K` nonzero state supports are pairwise orthogonal, and

`d>=K`.

This is sharp: take `d=K`, `rho_mu=sum_c mu(c)|c><c|`, and `E_b=|b><b|` for used outcomes, with zero operators for unused fine labels. The coarse output is deterministic postprocessing, and the independent waits can be appended as independent classical output randomness. This representation is affine in carrier distributions and respects the aliasing examples.

For a fixed noninjective `g`, the same argument on one representative carrier from each used fiber gives the sharp minimum `d=r` for the complete observable interface when all carrier inputs are included. The construction uses the diagonal states `sum_b(g_*mu)(b)|b><b|`. A selected finite preparation menu can have a lower requirement because the extreme output states may be unavailable. For example, if every available law has the same output vector `p`, a one-dimensional state with scalar POVM elements `E_b=p(b)` reproduces that restricted menu. Thus one cannot infer `d>=K` merely from the nominal carrier alphabet when the intended comparison only includes a smaller preparation menu.

Even with `M=2`, if one asks for a **single common carrier-state encoding** that reproduces all programmable binary singleton tests and all point carrier inputs, the sharp lower bound is again `d>=K`: the singleton test for each carrier has expectation one on its state and zero on all others, forcing the same pairwise orthogonality. This is a comparison of the whole test family, not a claim that a single binary native readout carries `log K` bits in one use.

The input-system comparison does not count the eight-site apparatus as a `d`-dimensional carrier. A fixed source-independent quantum ancilla, if introduced in the comparison, merely produces an effective POVM on the input and cannot evade the bound. A carrier-dependent ancillary preparation would be additional carrier-correlated input and must be included in the represented interface; the general conditional independence `alpha(A|C)` by itself does not forbid such side information. In the supplied fixed native apparatus the headers/program are fixed and the clock randomness is independent of the carrier.

### 4.6 What is and is not established about a qubit

Choosing `K=M=3` and supplying three point preparations gives a fixed-program native detector with three perfectly distinguished carrier states. Choosing `T=6` with the paired-label construction gives the same three states with genuine source aliasing. Larger finite `K` are permitted by the same construction pattern. These are explicit conditional witnesses against deriving a universal two-message carrier capacity from the four axioms alone. For a fixed `M=2` output-only experiment, the one-use capacity is nevertheless at most one bit; this comes from that supplied output alphabet, not from a derived quantum-state geometry.

The site possibility algebra is `M_2(C)`, but the construction uses distinct scalar matrices `cI` as record contents. They are not asserted to be trace-one positive density matrices, and the readout is not asserted to be a POVM or to follow Born weights. The algebraic presentation alone does not identify the operational preparation simplex, its effects, or its capacity with quantum states and effects on `C^2`.

For `K=2`, the full carrier geometry is a classical bit interval with classical effects; it embeds as diagonal qubit states but does not supply a Bloch ball or noncommuting quantum tests. For larger `K`, the full carrier simplex and available programmable sharp tests are correspondingly larger, subject to the finite-menu and fine-alphabet restrictions above. No Born rule, Hilbert-state geometry, unrestricted measurement family, tensor-product composition, or general quantum-capacity claim has been derived. The verified result is the bounded local realization and its explicitly classical carrier-mediated interface.
