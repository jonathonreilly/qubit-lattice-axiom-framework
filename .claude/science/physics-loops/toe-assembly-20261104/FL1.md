## 2. Cell group and lifts

**Theorem 1 (G: geometric inverter).** Every turn \(\gamma=(Q,t)\in\mathscr G\) fixes an even-cube centre \(p\in c_0+2\mathbb Z^3\). A half-turn \(\eta\in\mathscr G\) about \(p\) inverts it.

*Proof.* Write \(P(v_1,v_2,v_3)=(v_3,v_1,v_2)\). `γ^3 = (Q^3, (I+Q+Q^2)t) = id` gives `Q^3 = I`. If `Q = I`, then `γ^3` is the translation by `3t`, so `t = 0` and `γ = id`, against order three. So `Q` has order three. If `Q` fixed an axis line, `Qe_i = ±e_i`, and `Q` would act on the other two axes as a signed permutation of a plane, of order 1, 2 or 4. With `Q^3 = I` that forces `Q = I`. So `Q` permutes the axes by a 3-cycle: `Q = D_1P^{±1}` with `D_1 = diag(s_1, s_2, s_3)` and `s_1s_2s_3 = 1`, since `det Q = det P = 1`. For `D = diag(d_1, d_2, d_3)`, `DPD^{-1} = diag(d_1d_3, d_2d_1, d_3d_2)P`; take `(d_1, d_2, d_3) = (1, s_2, s_1)`. Also `DP^{-1}D^{-1} = diag(d_1d_2, d_2d_3, d_3d_1)P^{-1}`; take `(1, s_1, s_1s_2)`. Both give `D_1P^{±1}`. (referee closure, FL1_REFEREE.md) Its translation obeys \((I+Q+Q^2)t=0\). For \(Q=P\), \(t'=t/2\) has coordinate sum zero and \(v=(0,t'_2,-t'_1)\) solves \((I-P)v=t'\). Conjugation by \(D\), and the analogous calculation for \(P^{-1}\), gives an integral solution in every case. Thus \(p=c_0+2v\) is fixed. The half-turn \(H_0(v_1,v_2,v_3)=(-v_2,-v_1,-v_3)\) obeys \(H_0PH_0^{-1}=P^{-1}\). Set \(H=DH_0D^{-1}\) and \(\eta=(H,(I-H)(p-c_0))\). Its translation is even, it fixes \(p\), and its linear part inverts \(Q\). Both \(\eta\gamma\eta^{-1}\) and \(\gamma^{-1}\) fix \(p\) and have linear part \(Q^{-1}\), hence are equal. □

**Theorem 2 (K: point stabilizers).** If \(K\le O\) contains a turn \(Q\) and no inverter of \(Q\), then \(K=\langle Q\rangle\cong C_3\) or the tetrahedral group \(T\) of order 12.

*Proof.* Lagrange gives orders 3, 6, 12 or 24. Order 24 contains an inverter by Theorem 1. At order 6, \(\langle Q\rangle\) has index two and is normal, so conjugation sends \(Q\) to \(Q\) or \(Q^{-1}\); an involution exists. If the involution commuted with \(Q\), their product would have order six, which no proper signed permutation has; hence it inverts \(Q\). An index-two subgroup contains all squares. The eight order-three rotations and the three coordinate half-turns are squares, so an order-12 subgroup is precisely \(T\). Write `Q = R(2π/3, n)`, the rotation by `2π/3` about the unit vector `n`. For `h ∈ O`, `hQh^{-1} = R(2π/3, hn)`, and `Q^{-1} = R(2π/3, −n)`. A rotation by an angle in `(0, π)` determines its oriented axis: its fixed vectors span its axis line, and `R(θ, −n) = R(−θ, n) ≠ R(θ, n)`, since `R(θ, n)^2 = R(2θ, n) ≠ I`. So `hQh^{-1} = Q^{-1}` forces `hn = −n`: an inverter maps the axis line of `Q` onto itself. In `T`, the elements of `⟨Q⟩` fix `n`. A coordinate half-turn moves every body diagonal to another one; for example `diag(1,−1,−1)(1,1,1) = (1,−1,−1)`. A turn about another body diagonal permutes the remaining three diagonals cyclically, so it moves the axis of `Q`. Hence `T` contains no inverter of `Q`. (referee closure, FL1_REFEREE.md) □
