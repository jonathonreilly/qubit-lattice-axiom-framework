## 4. Bottom-up operator lemmas

The following arguments are finite-dimensional and written by hand.
Their constants have the stated uniformity only when their hypotheses do.

### P1. Constrained inverse and exact Gaussian algebra

Let \(K>0\) be a real symmetric precision on a finite Hilbert space.
Let \(Q\) map onto a second Hilbert space. All adjoints use their
specified inner products. Put
\[
G=K^{-1},\quad S=QGQ^*,\quad q=S^{-1},\quad
H=GQ^*q,\quad P=I-HQ,\quad G_c=PG.
\tag{2}
\]
Then these inverses exist, \(QH=I\), \(P^2=P\), \(QP=0\),
\(G_c=GP^*\), \(QG_c=0\), and \(KG_c=P^*\). Moreover
\[
\langle f,qf\rangle
 =\min_{Qv=f}\langle v,Kv\rangle.
\tag{3}
\]

**Proof.** If \(u\ne0\), surjectivity of \(Q\) implies \(Q^*u\ne0\).
Thus \(\langle u,Su\rangle=\langle Q^*u,GQ^*u\rangle>0\).
This proves existence of \(q\). Multiplication gives \(QH=Sq=I\),
then \(P^2=I-2HQ+H(QH)Q=P\) and \(QP=0\).
The formula
\[
G_c=G-GQ^*qQG
\]
is symmetric. It gives \(G_c=GP^*\) and all remaining identities.
For \(Qv=f\), write \(v=Hf+z\), where \(Qz=0\).
Since \(KHf=Q^*qf\), the cross term is
\(\langle z,KHf\rangle=\langle Qz,qf\rangle=0\).
The energy is \(\langle f,qf\rangle+\langle z,Kz\rangle\).
Its unique minimum occurs at \(z=0\). This proves (3). ∎

This ports the algebra of CMP 95 (1.102)–(1.107) and
CA-I 13638–13665. At complex backgrounds, replace adjoints by their
analytic bilinear transposes. The identities still hold wherever the
inverses exist. Positivity does not continue by that algebra.
Nothing in P1 bounds \(q\) independently of the fine mesh.

### P2. An energy lift gives a uniform Gram gap

In P1 suppose \(E\) satisfies
\[
QE=I,\qquad \langle Ef,KEf\rangle\le C_E\|f\|^2.
\tag{4}
\]
Then \(S\ge C_E^{-1}I\) and \(q\le C_EI\).
If also \(\|Qv\|^2\le C_T\langle v,Kv\rangle\), then
\(S\le C_TI\).

**Proof.** The identity \(QE=I\) gives
\[
\|f\|^2=\langle Q^*f,Ef\rangle
 =\langle G^{1/2}Q^*f,K^{1/2}Ef\rangle
 \le\langle f,Sf\rangle^{1/2}C_E^{1/2}\|f\|.
\]
Divide by \(\|f\|\) when nonzero and square. Inversion proves the
bound on \(q\). For the upper bound, substitute \(v=G^{1/2}w\)
in the trace inequality; it gives \(\|QG^{1/2}\|^2\le C_T\).
Since \(S=(QG^{1/2})(QG^{1/2})^*\), the claim follows. ∎

This is the uniform-layer specialization of Sussman–Alpoge,
SA 11473–11490. Their layered version keeps both length multipliers
\(\Xi\); dropping them would change the assertion.

**A fully rebuilt scalar instance.** Partition a four-dimensional
periodic fine lattice into unit blocks of \(m^4\) sites, with
\(\eta=m^{-1}\). Let the fine pairing have weight \(\eta^4\),
the coarse pairing weight one, and \(Q_s\) be the arithmetic mean.
Set \(K_s=-\Delta_\eta+Q_s^*Q_s\). Its form is the sum of
the squared physical differences and \(\|Q_sv\|^2\).
If this form is zero, every edge difference is zero, so the field is
constant on the connected torus; its block mean is zero, so it vanishes.
Thus \(K_s>0\).

For \(m\ge2\) use the account's test function (SA 792–819)
\[
\phi_m(j)=16\prod_{\mu=1}^4
 \sin^2\!\bigl(\pi(j_\mu+\tfrac12)/m\bigr),\qquad
(E_sf)(x)=f(B)\phi_m(x),\quad x\in B.
\tag{5}
\]
The geometric sum of \(e^{2\pi i(j+1/2)/m}\) is zero.
The identity \(\sin^2t=(1-\cos2t)/2\) therefore gives mean
\(1/2\) in each coordinate. Hence \(Q_sE_s=I\) and
\(\|E_s\|\le16\).

We give the mesh estimate, since mere finite dimension would not do it.
For interior sites, the second physical difference of
\(\sin^2(\pi t)\) is bounded by \(2\pi^2\). Indeed, its second
derivative has that bound, and a second difference is its integral
against a triangular kernel of total mass \(\eta^2\), divided by
\(\eta^2\). At a block end the sine-square value is at most
\(\pi^2\eta^2/4\); the next interior value is at most
\(9\pi^2\eta^2/4\). The stencil crossing the end is thus bounded,
after multiplication by \(\eta^{-2}\), by a constant times the
absolute coarse values of the two incident blocks. The other three
sine-square factors are at most one.
Summing the four coordinate stencils bounds
\(|\Delta_\eta E_sf|\) on a block by
\(C\sum_{B'\sim B}|f(B')|\), with the block and its eight
face neighbors included. Cauchy–Schwarz for these nine terms, followed
by summation with weight \(\eta^4\), gives
\(\|K_sE_sf\|\le C\|f\|\), uniformly in \(m\) and volume.
For \(m=1\), take \(E_s=I\). The inequality
\(|u-v|^2\le2|u|^2+2|v|^2\) bounds the unit-lattice Laplacian
by 16, so the same conclusion holds.
Consequently
\(\langle E_sf,K_sE_sf\rangle\le16C\|f\|^2\).
The trace inequality has constant one, directly from the form.
P2 now proves
\[
(16C)^{-1}I\le Q_sK_s^{-1}Q_s^*\le I.
\tag{6}
\]
The same estimate on \(K_sE_s\) also proves
\(Q_sK_s^{-2}Q_s^*\ge C^{-2}I\), by testing
\(\langle K_s^{-1}Q_s^*f,K_sE_sf\rangle=\|f\|^2\).
This is precisely the scalar squared-Gram argument in SA 781–844.
It is not the field-row operator \(QG_0Q^*\) of Balaban.

### P3. A Gram gap plus a kernel bound gives inverse decay

Use unit coarse coordinates, fixed finite internal dimension, and the
periodic \(\ell^1\) distance \(d\). Define the two-sided norm
\[
\|A\|_\rho=\max\left\{
 \sup_x\sum_y\|A_{xy}\|e^{\rho d(x,y)},
 \sup_y\sum_x\|A_{xy}\|e^{\rho d(x,y)}\right\}.
\tag{7}
\]
Suppose \(S=S^*\ge cI>0\) and \(\|S\|_\rho\le B\).
Then, with
\[
t=\min\{\rho/2,c\rho/(4B)\}>0,
\qquad \|(S^{-1})_{xy}\|\le(2/c)e^{-td(x,y)}.
\tag{8}
\]
These constants are independent of the torus size.

**Proof.** Fix \(y_0\) and let \(W_x=e^{t d(x,y_0)}\).
The triangle inequality gives
\(|d(x,y_0)-d(y,y_0)|\le d(x,y)\). Thus
\[
\|WSW^{-1}-S\|_0
 \le\sup_{r\ge0}(tr e^{tr-\rho r})\,\|S\|_\rho
 \le2Bt/\rho\le c/2.
\]
Here \(|e^z-1|\le |z|e^{|z|}\), and
\(r e^{-\rho r/2}\le2/\rho\). The Schur estimate
\(\|A\|_{2\to2}\le\sqrt{\text{row bound}\cdot\text{column bound}}\)
follows by applying Cauchy–Schwarz to each row and then summing columns.
Hence the same perturbation bound holds in operator norm.
The geometric inverse series about \(S\) now gives
\(\|(WSW^{-1})^{-1}\|\le2/c\).
Its \((x,y_0)\) entry is \(e^{td(x,y_0)}S^{-1}_{xy_0}\).
This proves (8).

The bound also gives a two-sided Schur bound for \(S^{-1}\) at
rate \(t/2\). The required lattice sum is bounded by
\[
\sum_{z\in\mathbb Z^4}e^{-t|z|_1/2}
 =\left(\frac{1+e^{-t/2}}{1-e^{-t/2}}\right)^4.
\]
Choosing one shortest representative for each periodic displacement
only decreases this sum. Bond directions contribute a fixed factor.

Finally suppose \(S_{\mathrm c}=S+F\), with
\(\|F\|_\rho\le c/4\). Conjugation bounds \(WFW^{-1}\)
by \(c/4\). A second inverse series gives
\(\|(WS_{\mathrm c}W^{-1})^{-1}\|\le4/c\).
If the matrix entries depend holomorphically on parameters, the inverse
does too on this domain, by the finite adjugate formula with nonzero
determinant. This is a complex perturbation argument about
a real positive matrix, not positivity of a complex bilinear form. ∎

This ports SA 11492–11501 with an explicit smaller rate. It proves
endpoint decay. It does not yet give local-data walk coefficients.

### P4. A local parametrix would construct the required walks

For matrix coefficients indexed by walks, require each walk's domain
to contain its endpoints, and to be connected. Let \(\ell(\omega)\)
be a length for which concatenation at a common endpoint is
subadditive. Suppose every coefficient is analytic on its local data,
has a datum-independent majorant \(a_\omega(x,y)\), and restriction
to a factor's read set stays in that factor's declared data class.
Use the norm (7) with \(e^{\rho d(x,y)}\) replaced by
\(e^{\rho\ell(\omega)}\), summed also over walks.

Let \(S,T,D\) be square operators on the same finite lattice.
If \(ST=I-D\), where \(T\) and \(D\) have such expansions with
norms at most \(B_T\) and \(q<1\), then
\[
S^{-1}=T\sum_{n\ge0}D^n
\tag{9}
\]
has a locally analytic walk expansion of norm at most
\(B_T/(1-q)\).

**Proof.** Expand each product before taking absolute values.
The union of two domains meeting at the summed intermediate index is
connected. Its read set is the union of the two read sets, and its
length is at most their summed lengths. For a row sum of a product,
sum over its final index first and then its intermediate index.
The bound is the product of the two row bounds. Column sums follow
in the reverse order. Thus the weighted majorant norm is
submultiplicative. The \(n\)-th term in (9) costs at most
\(B_Tq^n\); its sum is \(B_T/(1-q)\).
Each coefficient uses only its union of local data.
Uniform absolute convergence on smaller analytic domains proves
holomorphy by applying the Cauchy integral formula term by term.
Since \(\sum D^n\) converges, multiplying (9) by \(S\) yields
\((I-D)\sum D^n=I\). A right inverse of a square finite matrix
is its inverse. The asserted expansion follows. ∎

This is the mathematical content needed from a parametrix, not a
construction of one for Balaban's \(S\). In particular, coefficients
of the residual must themselves have the stated local majorants.

## 5. Deductions used after the operator construction

### P5. Analytic straightening of the finite constraint

Write the nonlinear constraint as
\(\mathcal Q(v)=LQv+C(v)\), with \(C(0)=DC(0)=0\).
Suppose, on a complex norm ball of radius \(r\),
\[
 \|C(v)\|\le c\|v\|^2,\qquad
 \|DC(v)\|\le c\|v\|,
 \qquad LQh=I,\quad \|h\|\le H.
\tag{10}
\]
Assume also that \(h\) and \(C\) preserve the real subspaces
when the background parameters are real.
For \(b<r/2\), \(cHb\le1/8\), and \(\|B\|<b\), there
is a unique solution in \(\|D\|\le4cb^2\) of
\[
 D(B)=C(B-hD(B)).
\tag{11}
\]
It is analytic, real on real data, begins in degree two, and has
\(D_2=C_2\). The change \(v=B-hD(B)\) gives the exact identity
\(\mathcal Q(v)=LQB\), and has local inverse \(B=v+hC(v)\).

**Proof.** If \(\|D\|\le4cb^2\), then
\(\|B-hD\|\le b+4Hcb^2\le3b/2<r\).
The map on the right of (11) maps this ball into itself, since its
norm is at most \(4cb^2\). Its derivative with respect to \(D\)
has norm at most \(2cHb\le1/4\). Successive substitution,
starting with zero, is a contraction. The difference of the
\(n+1\)-st and \(n\)-th iterates is bounded by a geometric
sequence with ratio \(1/4\), which proves convergence and uniqueness
in the stated ball. The iterates are analytic, with convergence
uniform on smaller parameter balls, so their limit is analytic by
Cauchy's integral formula. Real data give real iterates and limit.

Applying the same estimate with \(b\) replaced by any smaller bound
on \(\|B\|\) shows \(D=O(\|B\|^2)\). Substitution in the
Taylor series of \(C\) proves \(D_2=C_2\). Differentiating (11)
gives
\[
 D'(B)=(I+DC(B-hD)h)^{-1}DC(B-hD),\qquad
 \|hD'(B)\|\le\frac{2cHb}{1-2cHb}\le\frac13.
\]
Thus solving \(B=v+hD(B)\) is another contraction: for
\(\|v\|<b/2\) it maps the closed \(b\)-ball into itself and has
ratio at most \(1/3\). This proves the local inverse, including its
analyticity by the preceding uniform-iteration argument. Explicitly,
set \(B=v+hC(v)\) and \(D=C(v)\). For \(\|v\|<b/2\)
these lie in the stated balls and solve (11); uniqueness verifies
the inverse formula. Finally
\[
 \mathcal Q(B-hD)=LQB-D+C(B-hD)=LQB.
\]
This proves every assertion. ∎

This ports the contraction at SA 8257–8281, with explicit abstract
norm constants and the same inverse formula, crediting Sussman and
Alpoge, with Claude and Codex as authors of their text. It fills
the step left implicit on CMP 109 p.267, between (2.10) and (2.11),
crediting Balaban. The OCR's nonlinear and linear
uses of \(Q\) are separated here as \(\mathcal Q\) and \(LQ\);
the reconstruction is fixed by the displayed identity \(LQh=I\)
and by the surrounding equation for \(D\), not by its garbled

The lemma does not derive the Haar density, the determinant, or the

### P6. Cauchy estimates, the local fifth-order remainder, and level sums

First, if \(|F|\le A\) on a complex norm ball of radius \(r\),
at a point of norm at most \(r/2\) one has
\[
 |DF[v]|\le(2A/r)\|v\|,\qquad
 |D^2F[v,w]|\le(16A/r^2)\|v\|\|w\|.
\tag{12}
\]
For the first inequality use the one-variable circle with radius
\(r/(2\|v\|)\). For the second use two circles of radii
\(r/(4\|v\|)\), \(r/(4\|w\|)\). Their closed polydisc is
in the stated ball. Apply Cauchy's integral formula and take absolute
values; zero directions follow by linearity. Boundary equalities
are obtained by increasing smaller circle radii to these values.
In particular a response factor bounded by \(Ce^{-a/s}\) keeps
that factor after differentiating a localized kernel, with the
displayed radius cost. For every integer \(N\ge0\),
\[
 e^{-a/s}\le N!a^{-N}s^N\quad(a,s>0),
\tag{13}
\]
because the positive series for \(e^{a/s}\) contains
\((a/s)^N/N!\). This is the arbitrary-power implication in S4;
the required exponential response estimate is an input, not a
consequence of (12).

Here is the fifth-order deduction ported from Sussman–Alpoge,
CA-I 31163–31302, crediting them, with Claude and Codex as authors
of their text, and Balaban's (3.34), (3.48)–(3.54).
At level gap \(n\), let the old analytic kernel have bound
\(S_{i'}e^{-\kappa d_{i'}(X)}\) on its one-power hereditary class.
Use the actual background chart of S5. Assume its admission lemma
applies to every \(\tau\widehat B+B'\), \(0\le\tau\le1\),
with \(\|B'\|\le\alpha^{\sharp}_{3,i'}/2\). Suppose
\[
 B=\widehat B+Q',\qquad
 \|Q'\|\le\alpha^{\sharp}_{3,i'}/4,\qquad
 \|\chi_4 B\|\le b_iL^{-n},
\]
where \(\chi_4\) restricts to the inner cube. Put
\(s_n=\alpha^{\sharp}_{3,i'}/(4b_iL^{-n})\) when \(b_i>0\).
For every \(\tau\in[0,1]\) and \(|\sigma|\le s_n\),
\[
 \tau B+\sigma\chi_4B
  =\tau\widehat B+(\tau Q'+\sigma\chi_4B),\qquad
 \|\tau Q'+\sigma\chi_4B\|\le\alpha^{\sharp}_{3,i'}/2.
\]
Admission therefore makes the kernel composed with this curve
analytic and bounded by \(S_{i'}e^{-\kappa d_{i'}(X)}\).
Its fifth derivative at \(\sigma=0\) is
\(D^5_\tau[\chi_4B]^{\otimes5}\). Cauchy's formula gives the
bound \(5!s_n^{-5}S_{i'}e^{-\kappa d_{i'}(X)}\). Since
\(\int_0^1(1-\tau)^4/4!\,d\tau=1/5!\),
\[
 \left|\int_0^1\frac{(1-\tau)^4}{4!}
       D^5_\tau[\chi_4B]^{\otimes5}\,d\tau\right|
 \le S_{i'}\left(\frac{4b_i}{\alpha^{\sharp}_{3,i'}}\right)^5
           L^{-5n}e^{-\kappa d_{i'}(X)}.
\tag{14}
\]
For \(b_i=0\) the left side is zero. No requirement \(s_n\ge1\)
was used: each circle is centered on its own point of the real
segment. If \(\Theta_n\ge4b_i/\alpha^{\sharp}_{3,i'}\), this
is exactly the displayed \(\Theta_n^5\) bound in the account.

If the resulting expression \(F(t_\square)\) is uniformly analytic
on \(|t_\square|\le R_i=\alpha_{2,i}/(2C_s\delta_{i-1})\),
another direct Cauchy integral gives
\[
 |\partial_{t_\square}F(0)|
  \le\frac{2C_s\delta_{i-1}}{\alpha_{2,i}}
       \sup_{|t_\square|=R_i}|F(t_\square)|.
\tag{15}
\]
The marginal cancellation is an analytic identity; carry it out
before applying (15). Thus this differentiation supplies one small
factor to the whole remaining expression. A bound of that ratio by
\(C\varepsilon^{\mathrm I}_1\) must be supplied by the radii
choices; it is not a second independently available small factor.

Terms with a factor \((1-\chi_4)B\) are **not** included in (14).
CA-I 31271–31277 explicitly leaves their exponential response bound
to Balaban. With that bound given, (12)–(13) explain its conversion
to powers; no estimate for the actual response is proved here.

Finally, in four dimensions the volume multiplicity \(L^{4n}\)
and an irrelevant factor \(L^{-(4+\beta)n}\), \(\beta>0\), give
\[
 \sum_{n\ge0}L^{4n}L^{-(4+\beta)n}
   =\frac1{1-L^{-\beta}}.
\tag{16}
\]
Multiply the finite partial sum by \(1-L^{-\beta}\) to telescope
and let its final term tend to zero. In (14) use \(\beta=1\).
This proves the summation implication without supplying a missing
irrelevant power or a missing bound on \(\Theta_n\). ∎

### P7. The pointed quadratic Ward tensor and its own extractor

This is the SU(2) deduction of SA 12901–13270, ported with its
actual-response and absolute-moment premises. Credit: Ethan Sussman
and Levent Alpoge, with Claude and Codex as authors of their text;
Balaban, CMP 109 §§4–5, for the underlying marginal extraction.

Use positive unit bonds \((x,\mu)\) of \(\mathbb Z^4\) and
Hermitian color generators \(t_a\) orthonormal for
\(\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}(AC)\). Pairings are complex bilinear
after complexification. Let an individual pointed member have total
\(\mathcal G_z(P)=\sum_{X\ni z}G(X,P,z)\), normally convergent
with two derivatives. Each summand is locally gauge invariant.
Let the actual, twice differentiable, gauge-covariant pair \(P(B)\) satisfy
\(P(0)=(1,0)\), with its actual first derivative \(h\); in the
account \(hB=(HB,D_1^*D_1HB)\). Assume full hypercubic covariance,
including reflections, and unit translation covariance for this
same member. Set
\[
 F_z(B)=\mathcal G_z(P(B)),\qquad
 \mathcal K_z^{\mu\nu;ab}(x,y)
 =\sum_{X\ni z}D^2G(X,0,z)
       [h\delta^a_{x,\mu},h\delta^b_{y,\nu}],
\]
and assume for some \(m>0\)
\[
 \sum_{\mu,\nu,a,b,x,y}
 e^{m(|x-z|+|y-z|)}
       |\mathcal K_z^{\mu\nu;ab}(x,y)|\le C_m.
\tag{17}
\]
The derivative representation on finite-support fields is included
in the premise. Then the kernel is exactly the Hessian of \(F_z\),
is color diagonal, and its bulk second moment has the Maxwell form
derived below. This conclusion uses no positivity of the Hessian.

**Proof.** Constant adjoint rotations leave every local summand
invariant at \((1,0)\). A linear functional on one color coordinate
must vanish: rotation by \(\pi\) about each of two other color axes
changes the sign of that coordinate. Thus \(D\mathcal G_z(0)=0\).
The second chain-rule term \(D\mathcal G_z(0)D^2P(0)\) is zero,
and normal convergence proves that \(\mathcal K_z=D^2F_z(0)\).
For a color matrix of second derivatives, the same rotations kill
off-diagonal entries; rotations exchanging axes make the diagonal
entries equal. These rotations can be checked without a group
representation theorem: choose Pauli generators. Conjugation by
\(it_a\in\mathrm{SU}(2)\) fixes axis \(a\) and reverses the
other two, while conjugation by \((I+it_a)/\sqrt2\) interchanges
the other two axes up to sign. Their matrix products give all the
asserted constraints. Therefore
\[
 \mathcal K_z^{\mu\nu;ab}=\delta_{ab}K_z^{\mu\nu},\qquad
 K_z^{\mu\nu}=\tfrac13\sum_a\mathcal K_z^{\mu\nu;aa},\qquad
 K_z^{\mu\nu}(x,y)=K_z^{\nu\mu}(y,x).
\tag{18}
\]

For a finitely supported site field \(\chi\), differentiate
\(F_z(B^g)=F_z(B)\), first in \(g=e^{it\chi}\), then in
a finite-support field \(C\). At zero the first gauge derivative
is \(d\chi\). The mixed derivative of the gauge-coordinate map
is multiplied by \(DF_z(0)=0\). Hence
\[
 D^2F_z(0)[d\chi,C]=0.
\tag{19}
\]
This identity extends to polynomial \(\chi,C\), as an identity
of quadratic jets only. Indeed take
\(\chi_R(x)=\chi(x)\vartheta(x/R)\), with a fixed smooth compact
cutoff equal to one near zero. For degree \(q\),
\(|d\chi_R(x)|\le C(1+|x|)^{\max(q-1,0)}\), uniformly in
\(R\ge1\): on the cutoff annulus its extra derivative costs
\(R^{-1}\), and \(|x|\) is comparable to \(R\).
First use a single-bond \(C\) in (19), and pass to the limit by
(17). Then sum against polynomial \(C\), again absolutely by
(17). No nonlinear function has been evaluated at an unbounded
polynomial field.

Taking \(\chi=x_\mu t_a\) gives, for fixed remaining indices,
\[
 \sum_xK_z^{\mu\nu}(x,y)=0,
 \qquad \sum_yK_z^{\mu\nu}(x,y)=0.
\tag{20}
\]
Define
\[
 T_{\mu\nu,\kappa\lambda}
   =\sum_{x,y}K_0^{\mu\nu}(x,y)x_\kappa y_\lambda.
\]
For any symmetric matrix \(S\), the polynomial
\(\chi(x)=\tfrac12 x^TSx\,t_a\) satisfies
\((d\chi)_\mu(x)=\sum_\kappa S_{\mu\kappa}x_\kappa
+S_{\mu\mu}/2\).
Use \(C_\nu(y)=y_\lambda t_a\) in (19). The constant term
vanishes by (20); arbitrariness of \(S\), and then exchange of
the Hessian arguments, give
\[
 T_{\mu\nu,\kappa\lambda}=-T_{\kappa\nu,\mu\lambda},
 \qquad
 T_{\mu\nu,\kappa\lambda}=-T_{\mu\lambda,\kappa\nu}.
\tag{21}
\]
Translation covariance and absolute convergence give the bulk kernel
\[
 \Pi^{\mu\nu}(x,y)=\sum_zK_z^{\mu\nu}(x,y)
     =\pi_{\mu\nu}(y-x),\qquad
 \pi_{\mu\nu}(u)=\sum_xK_0^{\mu\nu}(x,x+u).
\]
Only this bulk kernel is a convolution. Equation (20) makes its
zeroth and first moments zero. Its second moment is
\[
 P_{\mu\nu,\kappa\lambda}
 =\sum_u\pi_{\mu\nu}(u)u_\kappa u_\lambda
 =-T_{\mu\nu,\kappa\lambda}-T_{\mu\nu,\lambda\kappa}.
\tag{22}
\]
To see both signs, expand
\((y_\kappa-x_\kappa)(y_\lambda-x_\lambda)\); the two pure
terms vanish by (20), leaving the two negative mixed terms.

Replace lower bond starts by midpoints
\(x+e_\mu/2,y+e_\nu/2\). The value of \(T\) is unchanged:
every extra term has one missing coordinate factor and vanishes by
(20). The value of \(P\) is unchanged because its zeroth and first
moments vanish. Signed coordinate permutations now act as ordinary
four-index tensor transformations, with one sign for each index.
Reflection of a single coordinate forces each coordinate label in
a nonzero entry to occur an even number of times. Equation (21)
excludes \(\mu=\kappa\) and \(\nu=\lambda\). Thus the two
antisymmetric pairs have the same unordered labels. Permutations
make all \(T_{\mu\mu,\kappa\kappa}\), \(\mu\ne\kappa\),
equal to a number \(b\); (21) gives the other value \(-b\).
Consequently
\[
 \begin{split}
 T_{\mu\nu,\kappa\lambda}
  &=b(\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta_{\kappa\lambda}
          -\delta_{\mu\lambda}\delta_{\kappa\nu}),\\
 P_{\mu\nu,\kappa\lambda}
  &=b(\delta_{\mu\lambda}\delta_{\kappa\nu}
       +\delta_{\mu\kappa}\delta_{\lambda\nu}
       -2\delta_{\mu\nu}\delta_{\kappa\lambda}),\\
 b[G]&=\sum_u\pi_{\mu\nu}(u)u_\mu u_\nu
       \quad(\mu\ne\nu).
 \end{split}
\tag{23}
\]
An alternating four-index tensor is excluded by a reflection;
proper rotations alone would not justify that exclusion.
The bulk Fourier series is analytic near zero by (17).
Its quadratic term, \(-\tfrac12\sum_{\kappa,\lambda}
P_{\mu\nu,\kappa\lambda}p_\kappa p_\lambda\), equals
\(b(|p|^2\delta_{\mu\nu}-p_\mu p_\nu)\).
In the action convention
\(A(e^{itB})=\tfrac12t^2\sum_p|dB(p)|^2+O(t^3)\), this
is the Maxwell coefficient. There is no extra color factor because
(18) averages the three diagonal color entries.

For fixed actual \(h\), (23) is linear in this member, ignores its
additive constants, and is continuous in (17): a quadratic weight
is bounded by an exponential weight times
\(\sup_{r\ge0}r^2e^{-mr}<\infty\). The latter finiteness follows,
for example, from the positive cubic term of \(e^{mr}\) for
\(r\ge1\), and the bound one for \(r\le1\).
Thus normally convergent sums and analytic member parameters pass
through the extractor. ∎

For completeness, (17) follows from the account's local sufficient
conditions as follows. Suppose a usable pair ball has radius
\(a_{\rm core}>0\), the local potential has bound
\(g e^{-\kappa d(X)}\), the actual response obeys
\(\|h\delta_x\|_{1;X}\le C e^{-\delta\operatorname{dist}(x,X)}\),
and the rooted domain count is at most \(C e^{\kappa_Dq}\)
for \(q\le d(X)<q+1\). Assume
\(\operatorname{diam}X\le C_M(d(X)+1)\).
Two-variable Cauchy (12) bounds the summand of the Hessian by
\[
 Cg a_{\rm core}^{-2}e^{-\kappa d(X)}
 e^{-\delta(\operatorname{dist}(x,X)+\operatorname{dist}(y,X))}.
\]
Since \(z\in X\), choose \(m<\delta/2\) and
\(2mC_M<(\kappa-\kappa_D)/2\). The two weighted spatial sums
cost at most \(C(d(X)+1)^8e^{2mC_M(d(X)+1)}\): enclose
\(X\) in its diameter box; outside it the remaining exponential
distance is summable, and inside it there are \(O((d(X)+1)^4)\)
sites for each argument. The domain sum is then bounded by
\[
 Cg a_{\rm core}^{-2}
 \sum_{q\ge0}(q+1)^8
       e^{-(\kappa-\kappa_D-2mC_M)q}<\infty.
\]
The series converges because its successive-term ratio tends to a
number strictly below one. The radius cost \(a_{\rm core}^{-2}\)
is retained. This proves the sufficient-condition implication,
not the actual response estimate or a radius-independent potential
norm estimate. Nor does P7 prove the cubic/quartic Ward completion
in S7, or the finite-window replacement in S8.

### P8. Connected logarithm, local union filing, and the retained amplitude

This ports the proofs at SA 1223–1373 and 14659–14772. Credit:
Ethan Sussman and Levent Alpoge, with Claude and Codex as authors
of their text; the Kotecký–Preiss criterion used there is credited
to Roman Kotecký and David Preiss. The following argument proves
the needed combinatorics instead of citing that criterion as a theorem.

Let \(\mathcal P\) be a finite polymer set with symmetric
incompatibility, each polymer incompatible with itself. For a list
\((X_1,\ldots,X_n)\), let
\[
 \phi(X_1,\ldots,X_n)=
 \sum_{\substack{F\text{ connected graph on }[n]\\
             \text{every edge joins incompatible polymers}}}
               (-1)^{|E(F)|},\qquad \phi(X_1)=1.
\]
If \(a_X\ge0\) and
\[
 \sum_{Y\not\sim X}|z_Y|e^{a_Y}\le a_X,
\tag{24}
\]
then the connected series
\[
 \mathcal L(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n!}
        \sum_{X_1,\ldots,X_n}\phi(X_1,\ldots,X_n)
                                   \prod_i z_{X_i}
\tag{25}
\]
converges absolutely and \(e^{\mathcal L}=Z\ne0\), where
\(Z\) is the sum of \(\prod_{X\in\mathcal A}z_X\) over
compatible subsets \(\mathcal A\), including the empty set.
The rooted absolute series with first polymer \(X\), weight
\(1/(n-1)!\), and no activity at that root is at most \(e^{a_X}\).

**Proof.** Order the edges of the incompatibility graph of a list.
An edge is eligible for an edge set \(F\) if its endpoints are
joined in \(F\) by a path of strictly smaller edges. Toggle the
smallest eligible edge, when one exists. Its eligibility and that
of every smaller edge are unchanged, so this operation is an
involution. It preserves connectivity and reverses the sign
\((-1)^{|F|}\). All such connected graphs cancel. A survivor
has no cycle, since the largest edge in a cycle would be eligible.
Thus survivors are spanning trees, all with sign \((-1)^{n-1}\).
It follows that \(|\phi|\) is at most the number of
spanning trees of that incompatibility graph. For \(n=1\) the
empty tree gives one.

To sum trees with root \(X\), start \(T_X^{(0)}=1\) and set
\[
 T_X^{(h+1)}=\exp\!\left(\sum_{Y\not\sim X}|z_Y|T_Y^{(h)}\right).
\]
Deleting the root leaves an unordered collection of child trees;
the \(r!\) in the exponential accounts for their ordering. For a
total of \(n-1\) nonroot labels, assigning labels among those
subtrees contributes the multinomial coefficient. It cancels their
factorials, leaving exactly \(1/(n-1)!\). Thus the displayed
iteration sums all rooted trees of height at most \(h+1\), with
the required labeled weights. All weights are nonnegative, so the
iterates increase. Equation (24) inductively bounds them by
\(e^{a_X}\). Monotone convergence and the tree inequality prove
the asserted rooted bound. Rooting an unrooted list at its first
vertex, and replacing \(1/n!\) by the larger \(1/(n-1)!\),
gives the absolute bound \(\sum_X|z_X|e^{a_X}\) for (25).

The formal identity \(e^{\mathcal L}=Z\) follows by partitioning
every graph into its connected components: expanding
\(\prod_{i<j}(1-\mathbf1_{X_i\not\sim X_j})\) sums all graphs,
and then all lists with factor \(1/n!\). Compatible lists have
distinct entries, so this is exactly the subset partition function.
Near zero absolute convergence makes the formal identity an
analytic identity. Apply it to \(tz\), \(|t|<1\), and use the
identity theorem to extend across this disc. The same summable
majorant holds at \(|t|=1\); radial continuity proves the identity
at \(t=1\), and its exponential is nonzero. Uniform majorants on
smaller parameter domains also prove holomorphy by termwise Cauchy
integration. This proves the finite statement. ∎

Here is its local application, including the amplitude. Suppose
actual activities \(H(Z;P_Z)\) are analytic on their declared
local cores, restrict hereditarily, and obey
\[
 h_Z:=\sup_{P_Z}|H(Z;P_Z)|
       \le\tau e^{-\Lambda\ell(Z)},\qquad
 \ell(Z)=d_c(Z)+5,\quad0\le\tau\le1.
\tag{26}
\]
The supremum ranges over local data, without requiring a global
extension. Assume \(\#Z\le C_v(d_c(Z)+1)\), and the rooted
count at distances in \([m,m+1)\) is at most
\(C_De^{\kappa_Dm}\). Incompatibility is witnessed by a cube
among at most \(c_{\rm adj}\#Z\) cubes of \(Z\) and its wall
neighbors. Fix an output rate \(\mu>0\) and write
\[
 g_*=\Lambda-\mu>\kappa_D+C_v,\qquad
 B_*=C_De^{2C_v}e^{-5g_*}
          \sum_{m\ge0}e^{-(g_*-\kappa_D-C_v)m},
 \qquad c_{\rm adj}B_*\le1.
\tag{27}
\]
For \(w_Z=h_Ze^{\mu\ell(Z)}\), summing the distance bins
directly gives \(\sup_q\sum_{Z\ni q}w_Ze^{\#Z}\le\tau B_*\).
Hence
\(\sum_{Y\not\sim Z}w_Ye^{\#Y}
\le c_{\rm adj}\#Z\,\tau B_*\le\#Z\), which is (24).

Assume the covering-tree geometry in the account: two incompatible
polymers can have their covering trees joined with length at most
five. A spanning tree on a connected list has \(n-1\) joins, so
\(d_c(\bigcup_iZ_i)\le\sum_i d_c(Z_i)+5(n-1)\). Therefore
\(\ell(\bigcup_iZ_i)\le\sum_i\ell(Z_i)\).
Define the local term at a core \(P_X\) by
\[
 E(X;P_X)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n!}
       \sum_{\cup_iZ_i=X}\phi(Z_1,\ldots,Z_n)
                  \prod_i H(Z_i;P_X|_{Z_i}).
\tag{28}
\]
Fix one cube \(q\in X\), and mark a list vertex containing it.
Every list in (28) has at least one such vertex. Thus marking
enlarges the absolute sum; symmetry changes \(1/n!\) to
\(1/(n-1)!\). Subadditivity of \(\ell\) and the rooted bound
then give
\[
 e^{\mu\ell(X)}|E(X;P_X)|
 \le\sum_{\substack{Z\subset X\\Z\ni q}}w_Ze^{\#Z}
 \le\tau B_*.
\tag{29}
\]
Keep \(Z_i\subset X\) when using actual fields; only the

## 6. One precise frontier, and three new attacks

### 6.1 LQ — the local inverse of the actual field covariance

The next lemma is the **uniform-layer subcase** of the constrained
inverse input CA-I 13142–13173. It already occurs on the full torus;
interfaces of different scales are not needed to state this frontier.
Passing it would not, by itself, settle the general layered case.

Fix \(d=4\), the group SU(2), and an allowed block factor \(L\).
Let \(\eta=L^{-k}\), \(k\ge0\), and use uniform physical
coarse block length one on a periodic lattice with room for the
early propagator cubes. Fine pairings have their physical volume
\(\eta^4\); coarse row pairings have volume one. Let \(Q(U)\)
be the **actual** field averaging row of CMP 98/99b, with the
CMP 109 symmetric contour convention and the exact physical
normalizations. Let \(D_U,R(U),\Delta(U)\) be the zero-current
operators at that same background. Thus, in this uniform geometry,
\[
 K(U)=\Delta(U)+D_UR(U)D_U^*+Q(U)^*Q(U),
 \qquad G_0(U)=K(U)^{-1},\qquad S(U)=Q(U)G_0(U)Q(U)^*.
\tag{30}
\]
These are CA-I 13583–13594 with \(\ell=1\). On complex data
the star is the analytic bilinear transpose, not conjugation.
Use the exact definitions of these primitives; replacing \(R\)
by the identity would change the problem.

**Lemma LQ to prove.** Choose early propagator size
\(\mathfrak M_*\), positive radii \(a_{0,*},a_{1,*}\), and
positive constants \(C,\tau\), uniformly in \(k\) and torus
size, before the later localization choices \(\kappa,M\) and
coupling ceiling \(\gamma\). At backgrounds
\(U=e^{i\eta\mathfrak a}U_r\) satisfying (S1), (S2) of
CA-I 12935–12973 with these radii, construct
\[
 S(U)^{-1}(y,y')=\sum_\omega q_\omega(U;y,y').
\tag{31}
\]
Here (S1) means local real gauges on the prescribed cubes of size
\(12\mathfrak M_*\), with
\(\ell_j|A|,\ell_j^2|\nabla A|<a_{0,*}\), and (S2) means
\(N_{U_r}(\mathfrak a)<a_{1,*}\). There is no constraint relating
a current to \(U\); the current in (30) is zero.

The terms must be words of local factors on connected lists of
early cubes. Each complete read hull includes the hulls of every
side branch. Each term must be defined and holomorphic on admitted
data on that hull alone, with consistent values when the same local
data are embedded in a larger torus. Give datum-independent positive
majorants \(a_\omega(y,y')\) for their norms and a connected
footprint length \(\ell(\omega;y,y')\), subadditive under
concatenation, such that
\[
 \max\left\{
  \sup_y\sum_{y',\omega}a_\omega(y,y')e^{3\tau\ell(\omega;y,y')},
  \sup_{y'}\sum_{y,\omega}a_\omega(y,y')e^{3\tau\ell(\omega;y,y')}
       \right\}\le C.
\tag{32}
\]
For definiteness one can use the length of a shortest unit coarse
lattice tree connecting all unit coarse cells met by the complete
read hull and the two endpoints; it does not count fine sites.
The elementary hull diameters are fixed early; changing between
this convention and a walk's successive connecting paths costs
fixed geometric constants. The factor three asks for a spare
exponential rate; it can be obtained by decreasing a proved rate,
and does not prescribe a numerical value of \(\tau\).
Both existence and bounds in (31)–(32) are part of the question.

This spells out the local-data form needed for subsequent products
and decoupling, rather than guessing the unreadable scale factors
of OCR (3.132). At \(\ell=1\) the scale multipliers of SA
11498–11501 are all one. CA-I 13158–13162 specifies local walk
hulls for this inverse; CA-I 5537–5553 spells out why the later form
construction needs local analytic coefficients and numerical
majorants, including at local data with no supplied global
extension. We have not replaced that demand with decay of the
resummed kernel.

### 6.2 Attack 1 — duality, smooth lifts, and inverse decay

Start with the exact Schur identities P1, then try to prove a
uniform inverse bound by a right inverse of \(Q\). P2 completes
the abstract reduction and the scalar averaged-Laplacian instance,
including the squared-Gram variant. It avoids a false inference
from finite-dimensional surjectivity. To see why that inference
would fail, even for the scalar block mean, lift a unit coarse
datum by a single fine-site value \(\eta^{-4}\). Its mean is
one, but each incident physical gradient has size \(\eta^{-5}\).
Its gradient energy includes \(\eta^4\eta^{-10}=\eta^{-6}\),
which has no uniform bound. The smooth profiles in (5) solve this
particular scalar problem. This calculation is by hand, not a
numerical experiment, and is not a counterexample to LQ.

There is a more relevant field-row construction in the outline.
SA 11460–11469 gives a flat lift, and 11643–11656 corrects local
gauge-frame seeds \(E^0\) by
\[
 E=E^0(Q_UE^0)^{-1},\qquad
 \|Q_UE^0-I\|\le L^{-1}+C_L(\epsilon+h)=:\vartheta<1.
\tag{33}
\]
Given the displayed seed comparison and seed energy bound, the
inverse is the series in \(-(Q_UE^0-I)\); its norm is at most
\((1-\vartheta)^{-1}\). The energy cost is multiplied by
\((1-\vartheta)^{-2}\). This proves the correction implication
without identifying a site lift with a field lift. Together with
the actual real positivity of \(K\), (33) would give the Gram
gap of P2. The outline separately retains projected Landau local
models and commutators at 11671–11685. In the repository, P82 I1
and I2 and P4-H-positive are exactly such named inputs; the ports
do not remove them by repeating this duality argument.

For this attack, even grant the strongest desired real gap
\(S\ge cI\), the exponential bound \(\|S\|_\rho\le B\),
and a sufficiently small complex perturbation. P3 then gives a
uniform analytic inverse and exponentially decaying entries.
It still does not furnish (31)–(32). Calling an entry of the
resummed inverse a term supported on its endpoints would be
incorrect. A finite example isolates the problem:
\[
 S(v)=\begin{pmatrix}1&a\\a&v\end{pmatrix},\quad
 0<a<\tfrac14,\quad1<v<2,
 \qquad (S(v)^{-1})_{11}=\frac{v}{v-a^2},\quad
 \partial_v(S(v)^{-1})_{11}=-\frac{a^2}{(v-a^2)^2}\ne0.
\tag{34}
\]
The inverse entry at site one reads the datum at site two. On an
arbitrarily large lattice the resolvent identity
\(\delta S^{-1}=-S^{-1}(\delta S)S^{-1}\) has the same
effect at remote sites. Decay bounds the effect; it does not make
it vanish. A valid local walk expansion must explicitly assign
that effect to terms whose read hull reaches the remote datum.

**Exact failure of attack 1.** Duality and weighted conjugation
construct a global analytic inverse with endpoint decay, conditional
on the actual Gram inputs. They give no expansion with individually
local reads or summable local-data majorants. Equation (34) rules
out the proposed endpoint-only filing. The actual field-precision
matching and local model hypotheses also remain to be rebuilt.

### 6.3 Attack 2 — spectral Neumann series and absolute paths

Try to recover walks directly from the inverse series. If
\(cI\le S\le BI\), choose \(b>B/2\); then
\[
 S^{-1}=b^{-1}\sum_{n\ge0}(I-S/b)^n
\tag{35}
\]
converges in operator norm. Expand every product into paths,
then expand its \(S\) entries into the available primitive walks.
This would preserve local reads, but operator-norm convergence of
(35) does not imply absolute convergence of these expanded paths.

The obstruction to that estimate is already exact in three
dimensions of matrix space. Let
\[
 S=\tfrac14I+\tfrac34\mathbf1\mathbf1^T
 =\begin{pmatrix}1&3/4&3/4\\3/4&1&3/4\\3/4&3/4&1\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{36}
\]
The constant vector has eigenvalue \(5/2\); its orthogonal
complement has eigenvalue \(1/4\). Thus (35) converges for
\(b>5/4\). But the entrywise absolute matrix
\(A=|I-S/b|\) has constant row sum
\[
 r_b=|1-1/b|+\frac{3}{2b}>1\qquad(b>0).
\tag{37}
\]
For \(b\ge1\), it is \(1+1/(2b)\); for \(b<1\), it is
\(5/(2b)-1>1\). Since \(A\mathbf1=r_b\mathbf1\),
the sum of the absolute weights of length-\(n\) paths starting
at any site is exactly \(r_b^n\). Their series diverges for
every choice of \(b\). This is an analytic calculation; no
eigenvalue computation is used as evidence. It refutes this general
proof method, not LQ for Balaban's operators.

One could regroup finitely many powers or larger spatial clusters
before taking absolute values, so (36) does not rule out another
walk construction. Such regrouping must itself prove that the
resulting local remainders contract in the majorant norm. That is
the purpose of the next attack.

**Exact failure of attack 2.** A spectral contraction supplies
cancellations within matrix products. The termwise absolute norm
required in (32) discards them. Equations (36)–(37) show that no
choice of the scalar preconditioner in (35) fixes this inference.

### 6.4 Attack 3 — local preconditioners and the boundary residual

Use Balaban's actual order for this repair: local inverse first,
commutator and domain-replacement errors second, then expand the
primitive propagators inside the errors. This is the construction
around CMP 99b (3.95)–(3.99), pp.411–413. That passage treats
\((Q'G'^2Q'^*)^{-1}\); the adaptation to (30) is the step
left unprinted before (3.132), p.422. The following algebra does
not assume that adaptation has already been done.

Cover the coarse lattice by boxes \(V_i\), and choose real
diagonal cutoffs \(h_i\), supported inside them, with
\(\sum_i h_i^2=I\), support overlap at most \(N\), and
\(|h_i(x)-h_i(y)|\le C_h d(x,y)/r\). Let \(I_i\) inject the
box coordinates. Seek local matrices \(S_i(U|_{V_i^+})\),
where \(V_i^+\) is a fixed enlargement, and set
\[
 J_i=I_iS_i^{-1}I_i^*,\quad
 T=\sum_i h_iJ_i h_i,\quad
 B_i=SI_i-I_iS_i.
\]
The exact residual identity is
\[
 ST-I=
 \sum_i[S,h_i]J_i h_i
 +\sum_i h_i B_iS_i^{-1}I_i^*h_i.
\tag{38}
\]
Equations (38)–(41), under the explicit bounds stated here, are
**Lemma P9, the quantitative local-parametrix residual**.
Indeed commute \(S\) past the first \(h_i\), insert
\(SI_i=B_i+I_iS_i\), and use
\(h_iI_iI_i^*h_i=h_i^2\). Summing the latter terms gives
the identity. Thus (38) checks the signs and the domain of every
factor without the OCR of (3.95).

There is a quantitative gain, not just a formal residual. If
\(\|S\|_\rho\le B\), then at rate \(\rho/2\)
\[
 \|[S,h_i]\|_{\rho/2}
 \le\frac{2C_hB}{\rho r}.
\tag{39}
\]
This follows from its entry
\(S_{xy}(h_i(y)-h_i(x))\) and
\(d(x,y)e^{-\rho d(x,y)/2}\le2/\rho\).
Suppose the embedded local inverses have a two-sided Schur bound
\(B_J\) at some common rate \(\sigma\le\rho/2\).
For the first sum in (38), fix its two intermediate indices.
A nonzero difference \(h_i(z)-h_i(x)\) requires at least one
of these two cutoffs to be nonzero, so at most \(2N\) indices
\(i\) contribute. Applying the row bound of \(J_i\) and then
(39) gives a row bound \(4NC_hBB_J/(\rho r)\). For a column
use the at most \(N\) indices with \(h_i(y)\ne0\), the
column bound of \(J_i\), and (39). The same larger bound works.

If the locally recomputed covariances also obey the interior
comparison
\[
 \|h_i(SI_i-I_iS_i)\|_\sigma
       \le C_{\rm tail}e^{-c_{\rm tail}r},
\tag{40}
\]
then the second sum in (38) has norm at most
\(NC_{\rm tail}B_Je^{-c_{\rm tail}r}\): for rows use the
left cutoff, for columns the right cutoff. Therefore
\[
 \|ST-I\|_\sigma\le
 \frac{4NC_hBB_J}{\rho r}
       +NC_{\rm tail}B_Je^{-c_{\rm tail}r}.
\tag{41}
\]
If these constants are uniform, choose \(r\) early to make
this at most \(1/2\). If the **expanded** residual has the
corresponding local walk-majorant bound, P4 gives (31)–(32) by
normal convergence. A kernel bound (41) alone is still insufficient;
one must retain the absolute expansions through its cancellations.

There are two concrete attempts to supply the local matrices.
Taking \(S_i=I_i^*SI_i\) inherits any global positive lower
bound by extending vectors by zero. P3 also bounds its inverse.
But this choice reads the whole background through \(G_0(U)\)
and \(R(U)\). It is not a function of \(U|_{V_i^+}\), so it
cannot be a local coefficient of (31).

Alternatively recompute
\(S_i=Q_iK_i^{-1}Q_i^*\) from a genuine local model, with
\(K_i=\Delta_i+D_iR_iD_i^*+Q_i^*Q_i\) and the model's
matched site/bond spaces and boundary conventions. Then its data
reads are local. A uniform lower bound for this \(S_i\) requires
a lift \(Q_iE_i=I\) with
\(\langle E_if,K_iE_if\rangle\le C_E\|f\|^2\), plus the
actual positive local precision. One must also prove (40), and
expand that difference into small local words. The global Gram
bound does not imply either assertion for a newly recomputed
matrix. The scalar profiles of P2 are not those field rows; nor
does deleting an exterior part of \(R\) compute the local
projection \(R_i\).

The original source identifies exactly the additional estimate:
at CMP 99b (3.97), p.412, it uses a difference of propagators on
two domains and gains the distance to the nearest changed datum.
The cited proof uses the local propagator constructions, before
the new inverse. For (30), the analogous estimate must include

continuations. Its coefficient cancellations must survive the

The account's later operator assembly does not resolve this

**Exact failure of attack 3.** The residual identity and its

coercivity but loses local reads; recomputation supplies local

### P10. Flat gauge removal and the coarse Schur form

Put \(\Delta_s=d_0^*d_0\), let \(R\) be the orthogonal projection
onto \(\Delta_s\ker Q_s\), and set
\[
 K_{\mathrm{fl}}=d_1^*d_1+d_0Rd_0^*+Q_1^*Q_1.
 \tag{44}
\]
This is the identity-background normalization of CMP 99b
(3.10), (3.21), (3.26). The image \(\Delta_s\ker Q_s\), not
\(\ker Q_s\) itself, is essential in (44).
For this paragraph assume \(Q_1\) is onto; P11 supplies it.

Then \(K_{\mathrm{fl}}>0\) at every finite mesh and volume.
If
\[
 S_{\mathrm{fl}}=Q_1K_{\mathrm{fl}}^{-1}Q_1^*,\qquad
 \langle f,A_{\mathrm{cl}}f\rangle
       :=\min_{Q_1a=f}\|d_1a\|^2,
\]
then
\[
 S_{\mathrm{fl}}^{-1}=I+A_{\mathrm{cl}}.
 \tag{45}
\]
Neither assertion here prescribes a mesh-uniform lower bound
for \(K_{\mathrm{fl}}\).

**Proof.** Telescoping the inner sum in (43), and using
\(n\eta=1\), gives
\[
 Q_1d_0=d_cQ_s.
 \tag{46}
\]
A closed fine one-form on a periodic rectangular lattice has the
form \(a=d_0\phi+h\), with constant \(h_\mu\). Here is the finite
argument. The sum of \(\eta a\) around a coordinate cycle is
unchanged when that cycle moves across one plaquette, since the
plaquette curl is zero. Subtract the constant field with these
periods. The remaining periods and plaquette curls vanish.
Integrate the remaining form from one fixed site. Interchanging
adjacent steps changes a path integral by a plaquette curl;
backtracking changes it by zero; a complete periodic cycle also
changes it by zero. Reducing paths by these operations proves
path independence. The resulting periodic function is \(\phi\).

If \(Q_1a=0\), (46) gives \(d_cQ_s\phi+h=0\).
Summing along each coarse cycle gives \(h=0\).
The coarse graph is connected, so \(Q_s\phi\) is constant.
Subtracting that constant from \(\phi\) makes \(Q_s\phi=0\).
Consequently \(d_0^*a=\Delta_s\phi\) belongs to \(\operatorname{ran}R\).
If the three squares in \(\langle a,K_{\mathrm{fl}}a\rangle\)
vanish, then \(d_1a=0\), \(Q_1a=0\), and
\(\Delta_s\phi=Rd_0^*a=0\).
Taking its inner product with \(\phi\) gives \(a=d_0\phi=0\).
Thus the positive semidefinite finite matrix (44) is positive
definite. This proves existence, without an unstated uniform gap.

For any \(a\), the vector \(Rd_0^*a\) belongs to
\(\Delta_s\ker Q_s\) by definition. Choose \(\lambda\in\ker Q_s\)
with
\(\Delta_s\lambda=Rd_0^*a\), and put \(a'=a-d_0\lambda\).
Equation (46) and \(d_1d_0=0\) give
\[
 Q_1a'=Q_1a,\qquad d_1a'=d_1a,\qquad Rd_0^*a'=0.
 \tag{47}
\]
The curl minimum exists: on the finite affine fiber its image
under \(d_1\) is an affine subspace, hence has a nearest point
to zero by orthogonal projection. Apply (47) to a minimizer.
For every fixed fiber \(Q_1a=f\), this proves
\[
 \min_{Q_1a=f}\langle a,K_{\mathrm{fl}}a\rangle
       =\|f\|^2+\min_{Q_1a=f}\|d_1a\|^2.
\]
P1 identifies the left member with
\(\langle f,S_{\mathrm{fl}}^{-1}f\rangle\).
Polarization proves (45). ∎

Credit Tadeusz Balaban for the projected gauge and constrained
minimum. The projection characterization is also proved in
the repository's p82, Lemma 2, lines 294–320. The argument above
uses it at its matched flat normalization. It does not port
p82's unresolved local-inverse assumptions I1 and I2.

### P11. A uniform gap for the actual flat field-row Gram matrix

There is a constant \(C_E<\infty\), independent of \(n\) and
torus size, such that
\[
 C_E^{-1}I\le S_{\mathrm{fl}}\le I .
 \tag{48}
\]
In particular, the finite positivity in P10 suffices to obtain
a **uniform coarse** gap; a uniform fine gap is not needed for
this deduction.

**Proof (port of the actual-row bumps).** This ports SA
11387–11439, (PX125)–(PX132), by Ethan Sussman and Levent Alpoge,
with Claude and Codex as authors of their text. The present
uniform layer has no adjacent-level leakage. We spell out
bounds on the discrete moments, so no finite computation
is hidden in the choice of profiles.

Let \(m=(n-1)/2\). For odd \(n\ge65\), use
\[
 \chi(u)=\bigl(1-16(u/n)^2\bigr)_+^2,\quad
 \beta=n^{-1}\sum_u\chi(u),\quad
 \sigma=n^{-1}\sum_u(u/n)^2\chi(u).
\]
Here \(x_+=\max\{x,0\}\), and the sum ranges over \(-m,\ldots,m\).
There are at least \(n/8\) integers with \(|u|\le n/8\).
There are at least \(n/16\) integers with
\(n/16\le |u|\le n/8\): the two intervals have total length
\(n/8\), and rounding loses at most two integers.
On these sets \(\chi\ge9/16\). Hence
\[
 9/128\le\beta\le1,\qquad 9/65536\le\sigma\le1/16 .
 \tag{49}
\]
The real function \((1-16t^2)_+^2\) is Lipschitz with constant
16. Its values and first mesh differences therefore have bounds
one and \(16/n\).

For a row \((y,y+e_\mu)\), support its seed inside the block at
\(y\), with only component \(\mu\) nonzero. Use the profile
\[
 \phi_+(u)=\beta^{-4}\chi(u)
       +\frac{m}{n}\frac{\beta^{-3}}{\sigma}
                         \frac{u}{n}\chi(u)
\]
in direction \(\mu\), and \(\chi\) in each transverse direction.
A fine longitudinal bond starting at \(u\) in that block is
counted \(u+m+1\) times by the right row and \(m-u\) times by
the left row. Thus their responses are respectively
\[
 \ell_+(\phi)=\beta^3n^{-2}\sum_u(u+m+1)\phi(u),\qquad
 \ell_-(\phi)=\beta^3n^{-2}\sum_u(m-u)\phi(u).
\]
Evenness gives \(\sum u\chi(u)=0\).
For \(\phi_+\) the sum of the two responses is one, while
\[
 \ell_-(\phi_+)
 =\frac mn-\beta^3\frac mn\frac{\beta^{-3}}{\sigma}\sigma=0.
\]
Hence \(\ell_+(\phi_+)=1\). No other coarse row reads the seed.
The lower bounds in (49) bound its amplitude by a fixed constant
and every first mesh difference by \(C/n\).
Its support has a fixed-width zero collar within the block.

For odd \(3\le n<65\), put the transverse profiles at their
center sites and use the two longitudinal positions \(-1,0\).
Their exact response matrix is
\[
 n^{-5}
 \begin{pmatrix}m&m+1\\m+1&m\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \det=-n^{-9}.
 \tag{50}
\]
Its inverse has entries of modulus at most \(n^5\).
Thus amplitudes are bounded by \(65^5\), and physical
differences by \(2\cdot65^6\). This is an explicit bound for
the whole finite range, not an enumeration.
For \(n=1\), use the identity lift.

Sum the seeds, one for each coarse bond, with its prescribed
color value. Formula (50) or the moment computation gives
\(Q_1E=I\). On any fine bond only a fixed number of seeds or
their one-cell difference collars occur. Indeed every seed
belongs to one block and one of four components; a difference
can meet only its two endpoint blocks. The fine volume of a
block is \(n^4\eta^4=1\). Squaring, using
\(|\sum_{i=1}^r v_i|^2\le r\sum_i|v_i|^2\) for this bounded
number of seeds, and summing gives
\[
 \|Ef\|_2^2+\|\partial Ef\|_2^2\le C\|f\|_2^2.
 \tag{51}
\]
The same count applies on periodic blocks.

Because \(R\) is an orthogonal projection,
\(\|Rd_0^*a\|\le\|d_0^*a\|\).
Every curl is a difference of two component derivatives, so
\(\|d_1a\|^2\le2\|\partial a\|^2\).
Every divergence is a sum of four backward differences, so
\(\|d_0^*a\|^2\le4\|\partial a\|^2\); periodic translation
identifies backward and forward difference norms.
Equations (44) and (51) imply
\(\langle Ef,K_{\mathrm{fl}}Ef\rangle\le C_E\|f\|^2\).
For \(n=1\), \(\|\partial f\|^2\le16\|f\|^2\), so the
same inequality holds after enlarging \(C_E\).
P10 now gives finite positivity, and P2 gives the lower bound
in (48). The upper bound follows from
\(\|Q_1a\|^2\le\langle a,K_{\mathrm{fl}}a\rangle\), again
by P2. ∎

**Exact outcome of new attack 1.** The flat Gram-gap input is
now proved for the actual straightened field rows, uniformly in

It does not complete LQ. At a nonflat background,

### P12. Site-projection algebra, derivative allocation, and the middle inverse

Use real finite spaces for the positivity assertions. Let
\(D\) be the site covariant derivative, \(Q_s\) the site row,
and
\[
 A_s=D^*D+Q_s^*a_sQ_s>0,\quad G_s=A_s^{-1},\quad
 T=G_sQ_s^*,\quad C_s=T^*T,\quad P=TC_s^{-1}T^*.
 \tag{52}
\]
Here \(a_s>0\) is the prescribed row mass and \(Q_s\) is onto.
Then \(C_s>0\), \(P\) is an orthogonal projection, and
\(I-P\) is the projection onto \(D^*D\ker Q_s\).

**Proof.** Since \(T\) is injective,
\(\langle v,C_sv\rangle=\|Tv\|^2>0\).
Multiplication gives \(P^*=P\), \(P^2=P\), with range
\(\operatorname{ran}T\) and kernel \(\ker T^*\).
If \(Q_s\lambda=0\), then
\(A_s\lambda=D^*D\lambda\), so
\(Q_sG_sD^*D\lambda=0\).
Conversely \(Q_sG_sf=0\) implies
\(\lambda=G_sf\in\ker Q_s\), and \(D^*D\lambda=f\).
Thus \(\ker T^*=D^*D\ker Q_s\).
This proves the assertion and shows that this projection is
independent of the positive row mass. ∎

This is the proof already given in p82, Lemma 2, specialized to
these names. Credit Balaban, CMP 99b (3.21), (3.24)–(3.26),
and that repository port. In the OCR of (3.25), the exponent
of the site propagator is ambiguous. Equation (52) fixes it:
the middle matrix is \(Q_sG_s^2Q_s^*\), not \(Q_sG_sQ_s^*\).
The former is required by \(P^2=P\); the preceding source
paragraph and CA-I 13557–13581 specify that same square.

Let \(\Delta\) be the field Hessian and \(Q\) its field row.
Put
\[
 F=\Delta+DD^*+Q^*Q,\quad V=DT,\quad
 K=F-VC_s^{-1}V^*,\quad
 B_s=C_s-V^*F^{-1}V.
 \tag{53}
\]
Suppose \(F>0\). Then \(K>0\) if and only if \(B_s>0\).
Where these inverses exist,
\[
 K^{-1}=F^{-1}+F^{-1}VB_s^{-1}V^*F^{-1}.
 \tag{54}
\]
These are exactly the projected \(K\) and an alternative
inverse construction; no field covariance inverse occurs in
the right member.

**Proof.** Complete the square in the real block form
\[
 \langle x,Fx\rangle+2\langle x,Vz\rangle+\langle z,C_sz\rangle .
\]
Eliminating \(x\) leaves \(\langle z,B_sz\rangle\);
eliminating \(z\) leaves \(\langle x,Kx\rangle\).
Since \(F,C_s>0\), positivity of either Schur complement is
equivalent to positivity of the block form.
Moreover
\[
 KF^{-1}V=V-V C_s^{-1}V^*F^{-1}V
          =VC_s^{-1}B_s .
\]
Multiplying (54) by \(K\) gives the identity. A square right
inverse is an inverse. The same algebra holds for bilinear
transposes on complex data whenever the inverses exist. ∎

There is also a useful quantitative comparison which allocates
derivatives before estimating them. Compare two models on the
**same** site and row spaces. In any submultiplicative,
transpose-invariant norm suppose
\[
 \|T_i\|\le B_T,\quad\|V_i\|\le B_V,\quad
 \|C_{s,i}^{-1}\|\le H,\qquad
 \|T_1-T_0\|\le e_T,\quad\|V_1-V_0\|\le e_V .
\]
Then
\[
 \|V_1C_{s,1}^{-1}V_1^*-V_0C_{s,0}^{-1}V_0^*\|
 \le 2B_VH e_V+2B_V^2H^2B_T e_T .
 \tag{55}
\]
Indeed
\(C_{s,1}-C_{s,0}
=(T_1^*-T_0^*)T_1+T_0^*(T_1-T_0)\),
so its norm is at most \(2B_Te_T\).
Use
\(C_{s,1}^{-1}-C_{s,0}^{-1}
=C_{s,1}^{-1}(C_{s,0}-C_{s,1})C_{s,0}^{-1}\).
Telescope the two exterior \(V\)'s and then the middle inverse
to obtain (55). The word norm of P4 has these algebra properties.
Thus (55) also holds there **if** the input differences have
such expansions.

This does not estimate \(D(P_1-P_0)D^*\) by multiplying an
undifferentiated bound by \(\|D\|^2\). That would cost
\(O(\eta^{-2})\). The quantities that must be compared are
\(DT=DG_sQ_s^*\) and \(T\), with the boundary spaces matched.

Here is an exact test of the proposed shortcut in (54).
In one dimension of matrix space let \(t>0\), \(D=t\),
\(Q_s=Q=1\), \(a_s=1\), and \(\Delta=0\). Then
\[
 T=(t^2+1)^{-1},\quad C_s=(t^2+1)^{-2},\quad
 P=1,\quad K=1,\quad F=t^2+1,
\]
but
\[
 C_s^{-1/2}B_sC_s^{-1/2}=(t^2+1)^{-1}.
 \tag{56}
\]
The normalized middle inverse therefore grows as \(t^2+1\),
although the final field precision equals one. All these
identities are scalar substitutions. This example is a test of
the proposed estimate, not a model of the actual lattice rows.

**Exact outcome of new attack 2.** Equation (55) reduces the

### P13. A grouped polynomial produces a contracting local residual

Let \(A\) act on a four-dimensional unit lattice or a periodic
quotient, with fixed finite internal dimension. Suppose it has
a series of holomorphic local coefficients
\[
 A(x,y)=\sum_\omega A_\omega(x,y),\qquad
 \|A_\omega(x,y)\|\le a_\omega(x,y),
\]
whose connected read hulls contain \(x,y\). Let \(l_\omega\)
be tree lengths covering their full hulls and endpoints, with
concatenation subadditive. Suppose
\[
 \max\left\{\sup_x\sum_{y,\omega},\sup_y\sum_{x,\omega}\right\}
       a_\omega(x,y)e^{\mu l_\omega}\le W.
 \tag{57}
\]
The numerical majorants must hold on each coefficient's local
data, not merely on one global background.
Restriction from a product's read hull to each factor's hull
must preserve that factor's declared analytic data class, as
in P4. The coordinate spaces and incidence maps are fixed.

Assume also, on the class of complete data under consideration,
\[
 \operatorname{Re}\langle v,Av\rangle\ge a\|v\|^2,\qquad
 \|A\|_{2\to2}\le b,\qquad 0<a\le b<\infty .
 \tag{58}
\]
All constants are uniform in mesh and volume.

For the conclusion on **arbitrary** permitted local data,
require this additional condition:

**LC.** Every local datum on the finite read balls of the
grouped polynomials below has a completion to a complete datum
satisfying (57)–(58). The completion preserves the lattice
incidences in the ball and every primitive coefficient read
there. Equivalently, it suffices to prove the polynomial bounds
(60) directly on those local data.

Then \(A^{-1}\) has a locally holomorphic expansion satisfying
(32), with constants determined by \(a,b,W,\mu\) and fixed
coarse geometry. If LC is omitted, the same construction and
bounds hold only for local data that are restrictions of the
complete class.

**Proof.** Let \(r\ge1\) be an integer, to be chosen. Keep the
terms with \(l_\omega\le r\), obtaining \(A_<\), and put
\(E=A-A_<\). For \(0\le\theta\le\mu/2\), direct summation gives
\[
 |E|_\theta\le W e^{-\mu r/2},\qquad
 \|E\|_{2\to2}\le W e^{-\mu r}.
 \tag{59}
\]
The bars denote the absolute coefficient norm of P4, including
full read lengths. The second bound follows from Schur.

Require \(W e^{-\mu r}\le a/2\).
Then \(\operatorname{Re}A_<\ge a_s I\) and \(\|A_<\|\le b_s\),
where \(a_s=a/2\), \(b_s=b+a/2\). These constants do not vary
when \(r\) is increased. Put
\[
 \lambda=2b_s^2/a_s,\quad
 \rho=\sqrt{1-\frac{3a_s^2}{4b_s^2}}<1,\quad
 N=I-A_</\lambda,\quad
 P_r=\lambda^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}N^j,\quad Z_r=N^r.
\]
For every vector \(v\), expansion of the squared norm gives
\[
 \|Nv\|^2
 \le\left(1-\frac{2a_s}{\lambda}
                  +\frac{b_s^2}{\lambda^2}\right)\|v\|^2
 =\rho^2\|v\|^2.
\]
Thus, with \(B_0=[\lambda(1-\rho)]^{-1}\),
\[
 \|P_r\|\le B_0,\qquad \|Z_r\|\le\rho^r.
 \tag{60}
\]
No self-adjointness of the complex matrix is used.

Every entry of \(A_<\) has range at most \(r\). Every primitive
read in it lies within distance \(r\) of its first endpoint.
Therefore each entry of either finite polynomial reads only
the ball of radius \(r^2\) about its first endpoint and vanishes
unless its second endpoint is in that ball. Treat each such
polynomial entry as **one coefficient**. Its definition uses
the local primitive series, not a chosen completion. Absolute
normal convergence of the short primitive series makes these
coefficients holomorphic. LC is used only to transfer (60)
to their local data.

Let \(V_r=C_d(1+r^2)^4\) bound the number of possible second
endpoints, including fixed bond species. Let \(H_r\) bound
the length of a coarse lattice tree covering that ball and
its read cells. We may take
\(H_r=C'_d(1+r^2)^4\): the ball is connected; connect its
finitely many cells by a spanning tree and include the fixed
cell-edge margins. Periodic identifications only reduce
the number of cells. Each coefficient of \(P_r\) is bounded
by \(B_0\), and each coefficient of \(Z_r\) by \(\rho^r\),
since a matrix block norm is at most its operator norm.
The same \(V_r\) counts first endpoints for a fixed second
endpoint. Hence both row and column coefficient sums obey
\[
 |P_r|_\theta\le V_rB_0 e^{\theta H_r},\qquad
 |Z_r|_\theta\le V_r\rho^r e^{\theta H_r}.
 \tag{61}
\]
This bound uses numerical majorants for the grouped entries;
it does not interchange a local-data supremum with a
cancellation-dependent row estimate.

Choose \(r\) large enough that, in addition to (59),
\[
 V_r\rho^r\le\tfrac18,\qquad
 WB_0V_r e^{-\mu r/2}\le\tfrac18 .
 \tag{62}
\]
Such an integer exists by a hand argument: the ratio of
successive terms of each sequence tends respectively to
\(\rho<1\) or \(e^{-\mu/2}<1\). From some integer onward
each ratio is below a fixed number less than one; the
remaining geometric sequence tends to zero.
Now fix
\[
 0<\theta\le\min\{\mu/2,\log(2)/H_r\}.
\]
Then \(|Z_r|_\theta\le1/4\) and \(|EP_r|_\theta\le1/4\).
Since \(A_<P_r=I-Z_r\), one has
\[
 AP_r=I-D_r,\quad D_r=Z_r-EP_r,\quad
 |D_r|_\theta\le\tfrac12 .
\]
Expand each residual factor into these two alternatives,
retaining every intermediate endpoint as a label. P4 gives
\[
 A^{-1}=P_r\sum_{m\ge0}D_r^m,\qquad
 \left|\{P_rD_r^m\}_{m\ge0}\right|_\theta\le4B_0V_r .
 \tag{63}
\]
For each product, the read hull is the union of its factor
hulls. They join at the summed endpoints. The union tree
length is at most the sum of the factor lengths. This is
the full-hull convention, not just endpoint distance with
an uncharged collar. Uniform convergence proves holomorphy,
and equality follows by multiplication by \(A\).
Torus consistency follows term by term from the primitive
coefficients and the same local ball construction.
Take \(\tau=\theta/3\) to obtain precisely the three-rate
reserve in (32). All choices precede later \(\kappa,M,\gamma\). ∎

The \(3\times3\) example (36) therefore does **not** obstruct
this method. There, only the ungrouped path series diverges.
Equation (60) retains the cancellations within a finite power;
(61) takes absolute values afterwards.

### P14. What a cutoff really completes

There are two useful completion statements. They are weaker
than LC for arbitrary connected read hulls.

First suppose a real connection is given on a cube \(V^+\),
with a **single** real gauge \(u\) there such that
\(U^u=e^{i\eta A}\), \(\|A\|_\infty,\|\nabla A\|_\infty<e_0\).
Let \(V\) lie inside \(V^+\), with a collar of physical width
\(w\ge1\), including one extra fine stencil.
Choose \(\chi=1\) on \(V\), zero beyond that collar, with
\(0\le\chi\le1\) and physical Lipschitz constant at most \(1/w\).
Then \(\widetilde A=\chi A\), extended by zero, satisfies
\[
 \|\widetilde A\|_\infty\le e_0,\qquad
 \|\nabla\widetilde A\|_\infty
                  \le(1+w^{-1})e_0\le2e_0 .
 \tag{64}
\]
For the one-cube conclusion one can choose \(V,V^+\) as
concentric cubes and use a distance cutoff. The discrete
product identity
\[
 \chi(x+\eta e_\nu)A(x+\eta e_\nu)-\chi(x)A(x)
 =\chi(x+\eta e_\nu)\bigl(A(x+\eta e_\nu)-A(x)\bigr)
  +\bigl(\chi(x+\eta e_\nu)-\chi(x)\bigr)A(x)
\]
proves (64), including the zero-extension stencils.
Define the extended connection to be
\(e^{i\eta\widetilde A}\), and undo an arbitrary extension
of the unitary site gauge \(u\). It agrees with \(U\) on
\(V\), and has a globally available small gauge.
This is the cutoff used in CA-I 47789–47812, with its
geometric hypothesis kept. Credit Sussman and Alpoge, with
Claude and Codex as authors of their text.

Second, let a real background \(U_r\) already be supplied
on the **whole** lattice. If a complex twist \(a\) is given
on a collar of a read set, with
\(\max\{|a|,|\nabla_{U_r}a|\}<e_1\), the same cutoff yields
\[
 \max\{|\chi a|,|\nabla_{U_r}(\chi a)|\}
             \le(1+w^{-1})e_1\le2e_1 .
 \tag{65}
\]
The covariant difference has the same product identity,
with one unitary transport; that transport has norm one.
Thus the cutoff map is bounded and complex linear on twists.
Composition with any holomorphic local coefficient is
holomorphic. This is the uniform-layer version of
CA-I 46800–46829, 46890–46930,
4.14:lem-local-balls. Its proof explicitly starts with a
global \(U_r\). It does not construct that real background
from arbitrary local real data. ∎

P14 gives genuine completion with a fixed radius loss when
the first read set fits in one small-gauge cube, or when
the real background is supplied globally.
It proves no gluing theorem for real gauges on a chain
of cubes. P13's polynomial read ball has radius \(r^2\);
neither (57) nor (58) forces that ball and its collar into
one of LQ's prescribed \(12\mathfrak M_*\) gauge cubes.

**Exact outcome of new attack 3.** P13 solves the absolute
inverse-series problem once the primitive local series and
the local spectral bounds are supplied. It cannot be dismissed
by the old spectral counterexample. The source proof in
inv-root is used, with a full-hull charge and LC made explicit.

For \(A=S(U)=QG_0Q^*\), this does not yet prove (57)–(58)
on the locally specified nonflat complex data of LQ.
P11 proves a real flat lower bound, not that complex bound.
P14 completes only the cases just specified. Substituting a
global spectral bound in (60) for a datum with no demonstrated
completion would be an unjustified step. The coefficients
are defined locally; the missing assertion is their uniform
local spectral control. The later CA sheet inversion takes
an existing primitive sheet as input; CA-I 47298–47321
explicitly sources \(G',G_0\) and their derivative sheets
from CMP 99b. It cannot create that input by inversion.

with a common holomorphic domain, uniform in \(k\) and the

\end{aligned}

The norms are sums of the norms of counting-coordinate color

**Proof.** Use exactly the finite maps \(F_W(a),u_W(a)\) of

\[
 \log(e^Xe^{-Z})=X-Z+E(X,Z),\quad
 |E(X,Z)|\le C(|X|+|Z|)^2,\quad
 |D_ZE|\le C(|X|+|Z|).
\tag{72}
\]
These estimates follow by multiplying the exponential series
and inserting the convergent series for \(\log(I+H)\).
After subtracting the constant and linear terms, every remaining
monomial has degree at least two. Differentiation removes one
factor; absolute convergence on a smaller fixed ball bounds the
remaining sums. The mean equation is

\(Z=\overline X+\operatorname{mean}E(X_i,Z)\).

Choose the ball so this map takes \( |Z|\le2\sup|X_i|\)
into itself and its \(Z\)-derivative has norm below \(1/2\).
Iteration is a contraction; the geometric difference bound
proves convergence, uniqueness and uniform convergence on smaller
complex balls. Applying the Cauchy formula to the iterates proves
holomorphy of the limit. These constants do not depend on the
number of paths. This is the proof of SA (QC5)–(QC7).

Each one-step path has at most \(C L\) bonds. Products, inversion,
the preceding mean and the remaining logarithms therefore define

\((W,a)\mapsto F_W(a),u_W(a)\)

on a joint raw logarithm ball of some radius \(r_L>0\).
On a smaller closed ball their first three derivatives have
finite bounds depending only on \(L\). Indeed these are finitely
many finite-dimensional analytic formulas; Cauchy circles inside
the larger ball bound every derivative. Define \(C_L\) to include
these bounds and the finite one-step incidence counts.

At identity the derivative of a contour mean is the arithmetic
mean of the path sums. In the plain average its two internal
terms are the difference of their block means at the endpoints.
The derivatives of the two \(u\)-factors cancel those terms.

times, each with weight \(L^{-4}\). This also covers a periodic
identification by counting incidences before identification.

\[

\tag{73}
\]
Also \(F_1(0)=0\) and \( |u_1(a)-I|+|u_1(a)^{-1}-I|

\tag{75}

Here and below suprema run over the backwards read sets.

\tag{76}
\]
Choose \(e_*\) so this is below \(3/2\) and \(2e_*<r_L/2\).

 |t_m-I|+|t_m^{-1}-I|\le C_Le,

\tag{77}
\]
This proof includes complex \(B\); it uses no real positivity.

\tag{78}

with relative row and column norms of \(E_m\) at most

**Proof.** We use flat physical differences \(\partial_\mu\),
and the physical pairings in §9. Write \(\|\cdot\|_{H^r_\eta}\)

\(
 \|V\|_{\mathcal A}=\|V\|_\infty+
                    \sum_\mu\|\partial_\mu V\|_\infty.
\)
The discrete product rule, with one shifted factor, makes this
a submultiplicative norm. The exponential and logarithm series
therefore converge in this norm in their usual small balls.

The difference between a covariant and a flat difference of \(a\)
is multiplication of a shifted \(a\) by
\(\eta^{-1}(\operatorname{Ad}e^{i\eta A_\mu}-I)\).
The exponential series bounds this coefficient by \(C\|A\|_\infty\).

\[
 B=(i\eta)^{-1}\log(e^{i\eta a}e^{i\eta A}),
 \qquad \|B\|_{\mathcal A}\le Ce.
\tag{81}
\]
For example, if \(\|U-I\|_{\mathcal A}<1/2\), the log

On the region where \(\chi=1\) it leaves \(U\) unchanged.

\[
 \|T\|_{2,\mathrm{fine}\to2,\mathrm{coarse}}
 \le\left(
       \sup_c\sum_b|T_{cb}|\;
       \eta^{-4}\sup_b\sum_c|T_{cb}|
       \right)^{1/2}.
\tag{82}
\]

\[
 \|Q^TQ-Q(1)^TQ(1)\|_{2\to2}
 +\|Q_s^TQ_s-Q_s(1)^TQ_s(1)\|_{2\to2}
 \le C_Le.
\tag{83}
\]
This verifies the measure factor that an unweighted matrix norm

\[
 \|(D_U^TD_U-D_0^TD_0)\lambda\|_2
       \le Ce(\|\partial\lambda\|_2+\|\lambda\|_2)
       \le Ce\|\lambda\|_{H^2_\eta}.
\tag{84}
\]
Equations (83)–(84) prove the required
\(H^2_\eta\to L^2\) bound for \(A_s(U)-A_s(1)\).

 |\langle v,(D_UD_U^T-D_0D_0^T)w\rangle|

\tag{85}
\]
To see (85), write the form as the pairing of the two

**The actual Wilson Hessian.** This ports SA (PX114)–(PX116),

\[
 D^2\mathcal A_p(U)[v,w]
     =\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}
             \bigl((\sum_rX_r)(\sum_rY_r)\bigr)
                +\mathcal R_p(v,w),
\quad
 |\mathcal R_p|\le C|P-I|
         (\sum_r|X_r|^2)^{1/2}(\sum_r|Y_r|^2)^{1/2}.
\tag{86}
\]
Here the factor \(1/2\) is the normalized color trace, not

#### P19. A dimension-independent real-point Taylor inequality

Let \(F\) be holomorphic on a ball of radius \(r\) in a
complex normed space, with \(\|F\|\le H\), taking values
in a complex Banach space. Suppose the real and imaginary
projections have norm at most one. If
\(w=u+iv\), with \(u,v\) real and

\[

                         +6H t^2/r^2.
\tag{109}
\]

**Proof.** Cauchy's formula on the complex line in direction
\(w/t\) bounds its degree-\(m\) coefficient by \(H/r^m\).
Summing the geometric tail for \(m\ge2\) gives
\(\|R(w)\|\le2Ht^2/r^2\), where
\(F(w)=F(0)+DF(0)w+R(w)\). The same bound holds for
\(u,v\). Complex linearity of \(DF(0)\) gives the exact
identity
\[
 F(w)=F(u)+iF(v)-iF(0)+R(w)-R(u)-iR(v).
\]
Taking norms proves (109). No dimension-dependent coordinate
polydisc or sum of partial derivatives was used. This is the
calculation behind SA 9899–9905, written with its remainder. ∎

#### P20. The quadratic-radius tower estimate

Under (104)–(105), at sufficiently small individual radii in
the stated order, there is an early constant \(K\) such that
the actual branches exist and
\[
 \begin{split}
 |\partial T_n(W)-I|&\le K(\epsilon_0+\epsilon_1^2)\eta^2,\\
 |J_n(T_n(W))|&\le K(\epsilon_0+\epsilon_1^2)s^2
                  \quad\text{on }Z^{\wr p}.
 \end{split}
\tag{110}
\]
The same conclusion holds with \(W\) replaced by \(U_r\)
and \(\epsilon_1=0\). The constants are uniform in both
tower rules, depth, mesh and volume. This ports SA's Theorem
5(a), 9746–9753, by supplying the real-point calculation and
its inputs from the repository. It asserts (110), not (106).

**Proof, first the input curl.** Write \(a=a_R+ia_I\),
where both parts are real Lie-algebra fields. Since \(U_r\)
is real, its adjoint transports commute with these projections.
Expand the four perturbed plaquette links as in (86). The
linear terms are the transported covariant curl; every remaining
term has two insertions, or one insertion and one reference
plaquette defect. More explicitly, move each exponential to the
initial corner by the intervening reference links, multiply
the four exponential series, and subtract their linear sum.
The four prefixes are unitary. The sum of terms with two or
more insertions is at most \(C\eta^2|a|^2\). Moving the
last reversed-link insertion to the convention for the ordinary
covariant curl contributes at most
\(C\eta|a|\,|\partial U_r-I|\). Consequently
\[
 \partial W(\partial U_r)^{-1}
   =I+i\eta^2 d_{U_r}a+E,
 \qquad |E|\le C\eta^2\epsilon_1^2+
                      C\eta^3\epsilon_0\epsilon_1.
\tag{111}
\]
This is SA 9905–9909's plaquette formula, derived from the
finite product rather than imposed as a new hypothesis.
The two curvature assumptions in (104), and boundedness of
\((\partial U_r)^{-1}\), imply
\[
 |d_{U_r}a|\le C(\epsilon_0+\epsilon_1^2).
\tag{112}
\]
Taking real and imaginary parts gives the same bound for
\(d_{U_r}a_R,d_{U_r}a_I\). Apply (111) to either real
part separately. The two real fields
\(e^{i\eta a_R}U_r,e^{i\eta a_I}U_r\) have plaquette
defects at most \(C(\epsilon_0+\epsilon_1^2)\eta^2\).
Their averages stay on the admitted real branches by (107).

**The holomorphic ball actually used.** Normalize this tower's
top length \(s\) to one. A field argument becomes \(w=sa\),
with derivative in the new coordinates scaled by \(s^2\).
Use the maximum of these value and first-difference norms.
The argument in (107), with a variable complex \(w\),
shows that its plain raw datum increment is holomorphic and
bounded by \(C\|w\|\) on a fixed ball. It applies to every
row of length at most one; bare rows have increment
\(\eta_n w\). The reference errors are
\(O(\epsilon_0s^2)\), and their summation is the same
geometric sum. Choose this ball small enough that its image
lies strictly inside the uniform raw-datum ball of
`a1-balaban` §9.1. This choice depends on the earlier finite
mean and background constants, not on \(p,n\) or the number
of rows. Its conjugating node factors have bounded condition
numbers by the product estimate in that section.

For each fixed plaquette or current coordinate in the top
layer, let \(F(w)\) be respectively
\(\eta_n^{-2}(\partial T_n(e^{i\eta_n w}U_r)-I)\) or
the level-\(n\) current. The just-described composition of
the actual average with the holomorphic critical representative
defines \(F\) on a ball of fixed radius \(r>0\).
`a1-balaban` (9.1)–(9.2), including its second-order estimate,
bounds \(\|F\|\) there by a fixed \(H\). This is a
crude bound; it is not presumed to have the factor
\(\epsilon_0\). The real critical-orbit theorem and its
plaquette-averaging input give
\[
 \|F(0)\|,\ \|F(sa_R)\|,\ \|F(sa_I)\|
       \le C s^2(\epsilon_0+\epsilon_1^2).
\tag{113}
\]
Indeed in these units the three real input curvatures are
bounded by \(C s^2(\epsilon_0+\epsilon_1^2)\); all
layered datum tests obey that bound, with the fixed adjacent
scale factors absorbed in \(C\). The real theorem is
linear in this datum bound. Real uniqueness identifies its
representative with the continuation used to define \(F\).

Now \(\|sa\|\le s\epsilon_1\). P19 bounds \(F(sa)\)
by (113) plus \(6H s^2\epsilon_1^2/r^2\). This proves
the second inequality of (110). Multiplying its plaquette
version by \(\eta_n^2\), and using
\(\eta_n^2s^2=\eta^2\), proves the first. All logarithm,
averaging and continuation ceilings used above can be imposed
on the individual input radii after \(M,B^{(109)}\) have
been fixed; none requires choosing an early constant as a
function of \(\epsilon_1/\epsilon_0\).

The top-layer estimates apply on the required interior.
CA-I 34178–34189 checks exactly this geometry: the maximum
tower collar has width at most \(R_1M_1\), while the two
removed layers have depth \(2M/L\ge4R_1M_1\). Thus
\(Z^{\wr p}\subset\Omega_n^{\mathfrak r}(Z)\), with
the current's stencils supplied. This holds for both rules.
No current assumption on the independent input variable has
been used. Taking \(a=0\) gives the real-factor conclusion. ∎

**Why the second attack still fails to prove L17.** Under a
change of label the new term is
\(K\mu^2\alpha_1'^2\), hence it is at most
\(K\mu\alpha_1'^2\). This is the right one-power scaling.
It proves hereditary admission with the additional sufficient
budget \(K(\alpha_0'+\alpha_1'^2)\le\alpha_0\), as in
the outline. It does not imply the CA-I budget
\(2B_3\alpha_0'\le\alpha_0\). The missing cancellation
is the quadratic term itself, not a level-summation factor.
Section 11.4 tests whether a curvature-based gauge removes it.

### 11.4 Third attack: remove the flat part by a complex local gauge

The quadratic term in (110) can be an artefact of taking real
and imaginary parts. For example, let \(U_r=I\), take a constant
commuting complex potential in one direction, and put the other
components equal to zero. Every plaquette of \(W\) is exactly
\(I\), even when \(\epsilon_1^2/\epsilon_0\) is arbitrarily
large. The input satisfies (104) for every positive
\(\epsilon_0\). It is critical with zero current and has its
own prescribed averages. For a real value of its one scalar
parameter, real orbit uniqueness identifies its axial
representative with the tower's representative. The finite axial
gauge and both representatives are holomorphic near zero; the
one-variable identity theorem gives the same identity for a
small complex parameter. Thus its tower curvature and current
are **zero**. This example refutes neither (106) nor (110).
It proves that the hypotheses do not imply
\(\epsilon_1^2\le C\epsilon_0\), and that the extra term in
(110) cannot simply be declared necessary for the actual map.

This motivates a third route: use complex gauge covariance to
remove the possibly large flat part before estimating the
critical field. The following finite calculation is what this
route supplies without assuming the desired estimate.

#### P21. The curvature-based axial bound, and its first-derivative limitation

On a contractible coordinate cube of physical side \(R\),
assume \(W=e^{i\eta a}U_r\), \(U_r\) real,
\(|a|\le\epsilon_1\) and
\(|\partial W-I|\le\epsilon_0\eta^2\). The coordinate-tree
gauge, normalized at a corner, has condition number at most
\(\exp(CR\epsilon_1)\). At sufficiently small radii its
link logarithm satisfies
\[
 W^u=e^{i\eta A},\qquad
 |A|\le C R\epsilon_0 e^{CR\epsilon_1}.
\tag{114}
\]
There is no corresponding mesh-uniform bound
\(|\nabla A|\le C_R\epsilon_0\) for this specified gauge,
even if the input \(|a|,|\nabla a|\) is uniformly small.

**Proof of (114).** Each real link has norm and inverse norm
one; multiplying by \(e^{i\eta a}\) bounds each by
\(e^{\eta\epsilon_1}\). A coordinate-tree path has physical
length at most \(dR\), giving the stated condition number.
For a link in direction \(\mu\), compare its two ordered
coordinate paths from the corner. Move its one extra step past
the coordinate segments after direction \(\mu\). Each move
crosses one elementary plaquette. The resulting strip has at
most \(C R/\eta\) plaquettes, not an area of order
\((R/\eta)^2\). Thus its holonomy is a product of at most
that many conjugated plaquette matrices. All conjugating paths
have length at most \(CR\), so the defect of each factor is
at most \(e^{CR\epsilon_1}\epsilon_0\eta^2\).

For matrices \(I+E_l\), the telescoping product estimate gives
\[
 \left|\prod_l(I+E_l)-I\right|
       \le\prod_l(1+|E_l|)-1
       \le e^{\sum_l|E_l|}-1.
\]
The sum is at most \(C R\eta\epsilon_0e^{CR\epsilon_1}\).
Make it smaller than a fixed logarithm radius. The series for
\(\log(I+E)\) is then bounded by twice \(|E|\); division
by \(\eta\) proves (114). This is a complex version of the
elementary plaquette telescoping in Balaban's gauge argument.
It requires no positivity or global gauge extension.

**The first-derivative obstruction is explicit.** Work in a
two-dimensional coordinate plane of the four-dimensional cube.
Let \(H=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\), \(\sigma_r=(-1)^r\),
\(x_1=r\eta,x_2=t\eta\), and choose \(\varepsilon>0\)
small. Set
\[
 U_r=I,\qquad a_2(r,t)=i\varepsilon\eta\sigma_r H,
 \qquad a_1=a_3=a_4=0.
\tag{115}
\]
All matrices commute. The input has
\(|a|\le\varepsilon\eta\) and
\(|\partial_1a_2|=2\varepsilon\); the other first
differences vanish. Its only nontrivial plaquettes are
\(e^{2\varepsilon\eta^2\sigma_rH}\), whose defects are
at most \(4\varepsilon\eta^2\) when
\(2\varepsilon\eta^2\le\log2\). In particular (104)
holds with \(\epsilon_0=8\varepsilon\) and any
\(\epsilon_1>2\varepsilon\), and the real gauge clause
is trivial. An independent current of zero is allowed.

For the coordinate paths taking direction 1 before direction 2,
the corner transport is
\(u(r,t)=e^{-\varepsilon\eta^2t\sigma_rH}\).
Direct multiplication gives
\[
 (W^u)_2=I,\qquad
 (W^u)_1=e^{-2\varepsilon\eta^2t\sigma_rH},\qquad
 A_1=2i\varepsilon x_2\sigma_rH.
\tag{116}
\]
Its first difference has norm
\[
                         |\partial_1 A_1|
                             =4\varepsilon x_2/\eta.
\tag{117}
\]
At any fixed positive \(x_2\) this is unbounded relative
to \(\epsilon_0\) as \(\eta\) decreases. The link
logarithms remain in their small branch, so no branch choice
explains the loss. This proves the stated limitation of the
axial construction. It is not a counterexample to the existence
of a different regular gauge, or to L17. ∎

**Attempt to use the current and reset.** The next proposed
step is the reset of `a1-balaban` §6, (6.1)–(6.6), with
complex rather than real local gauges. At reference one, its
nonlinear equation is still
\[
 A=A_1+HB-H\mathcal D(A_1+HB),\qquad
 A_1=-\mathfrak G V'(A_1+HB).
\tag{118}
\]
The near datum would be bounded by the input curvature; the
far datum would pay the fixed exponential collar factor.
If the complex critical field could first be placed in the
required local first-order slice at radius \(C Rq\), where
\(q\) bounds its curvature and physical current, the same
small quadratic remainder could be absorbed. With the
condition numbers tracked, this is a plausible way to remove
\(\epsilon_1^2\) by iterating a reset on the same field.

Here the elementary axial construction stops short. Equation
(114) controls values only. The reset uses both value and
first-difference norms in the slice equation and then recovers
the special second derivatives from its Euler equation. The
physical current of the **input** in (104) is unrestricted;
the free input variable \(\mathbf J\) cannot be used as
that current. Even for the already constructed output, P20
only supplies a radius of order
\(\epsilon_0+\epsilon_1^2\). To improve it by this route
one must prove the complex local gauge-and-reset estimate,
with the actual boundary data and node normalization, at that
radius. The real gauge result cited at CA-I 35201, or the
identity theorem applied to its existence statement, does not
supply those quantitative complex bounds.

We also tested whether the new mixed-system inverse alone pays
for this step. It does not: P18 bounds energy and dual-energy
maps. A scalar field equal to \(\eta^{-1}\) at one fine
site and zero elsewhere has physical \(L^2\) norm \(\eta\)
and first-difference \(L^2\) norm bounded by a dimensional
constant in four dimensions. Its supremum is \(\eta^{-1}\)
and its first-difference supremum is \(\eta^{-2}\). This
hand count rules out the needed inference from an energy
bound to a first-order supremum bound uniformly in the mesh.
The nonlinear equation needs the stronger, correctly gauged
estimate; relabeling the energy norm does not give it.

**Why the third attack fails.** Curvature telescoping does
remove the flat part in the value norm, but (117) shows the
specific lost derivative. A proof of the complex regular-gauge
reset would have to use the physical current and the elliptic
equation to recover that derivative while retaining uniform
node/frame and boundary bounds. This continuation has not
proved that step, and neither a bare axial gauge nor P18
justifies it. Consequently (118) has not yielded (106).
The failure is a specified missing estimate, not evidence
that (106) is false.

### 11.5 Constants, dependencies and the stopping point

P15 fixes the finite-mean constants at fixed \(L\), before
its radius. P16 adds only fixed coefficient and physical
pairing constants. P17 uses the flat supplier's already
ordered localization size and gap; its graded collar has
bounded physical width. P18 then fixes \(R\),
\(\mathfrak M_*\), the real and complex ceilings, and a
smaller positive tree rate. None of these depends on the
later \(\kappa,M,\gamma\).

For P19–P20 the real-orbit and analytic-ball constants precede
the small-pair radii. The later \(M,B^{(109)}\) enter
ceilings and the printed collar requirements, not a bound on
\(\epsilon_1/\epsilon_0\). The additional quadratic
budget following (110) is a proved sufficient condition; it
is not inserted into the theorem of §1.1 or into L17.
The coupling ceiling remains last throughout.

The three new attacks on the same L17 target are §11.2
(raw analytic continuation), §11.3 (real-point interpolation),
and §11.4 (complex curvature gauge and reset). Their respective
unremoved terms are \(\epsilon_1/s\),
\(\epsilon_1^2\), and the unbounded first-difference cost
of the proposed axial gauge. The second proves the useful
weaker estimate (110); the third proves (114) and locates
the derivative it does not control. These are new attacks
after the previous LQ frontier was closed, not a recounting
of the six attacks in the earlier rounds.

Only `library/b109.md` is changed. Source retrieval, text search,
formatting edits and file checks are not mathematical evidence.
No numerical, symbolic or enumerative run discharges any step.
The new arguments have not been checked by another provider.
