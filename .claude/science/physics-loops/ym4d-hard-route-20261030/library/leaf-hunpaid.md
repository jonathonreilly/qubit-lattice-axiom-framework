### Route 2: bound the number of surviving source pieces by finite differences

The proposed proof was a uniform bound on the source order of each surviving scalar occurrence.

Here is a hand test of the attempted truncation.
It tests that inference, not a Yang–Mills occurrence.
For each integer $`n\ge1`$, put

```math
 r_n=\frac1{4n},\qquad
 F_n(y_1,\ldots,y_n)
 =\log\left(1-r_n\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\right),\qquad |y_i|<2.
 \tag{6}
```

Use the branch with $`F_n(0)=0`$.
The argument of the logarithm differs from one by less than $`1/2`$.
Its power series therefore gives $`|F_n|\le\log2`$, uniformly in $`n`$ on these fixed coordinate discs.

Take $`0<\varepsilon\le1`$ and use the $`n`$ distinct shifts $`\varepsilon e_i`$.
Iterating the fundamental theorem of calculus gives their mixed difference exactly:

```math
 \begin{aligned}
 \sum_{J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{n-|J|}
         F_n(\varepsilon\mathbf1_J)
 &=- (n-1)!(r_n\varepsilon)^n
   \int_{[0,1]^n}
    \frac{dt_1\cdots dt_n}
    {\left(1-r_n\varepsilon\sum_i t_i\right)^n}
 <0.
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{7}
```

The denominator is at least $`3/4`$ before taking its power.
The integrand is positive. Thus no numerical calculation is involved.
There are nonzero mixed differences of arbitrarily high order despite uniform holomorphy and a uniform scalar bound.

This rules out a finite-order conclusion from those analytic facts alone.
It does not show that the specific top occurrence has these coefficients or unpaid masks.
