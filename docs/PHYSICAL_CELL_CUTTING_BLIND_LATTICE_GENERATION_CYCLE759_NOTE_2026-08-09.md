## What this answers

The object is the unit four-cube on sixteen corners, cut into least-volume
pieces at the adjacency cost floor. There are 15800 such cuttings; between
them they draw on 192 pieces, 24 pieces to a cutting, and each piece lies on
1975 cuttings. A vector on the pieces is *blind* to the cuttings when every
cutting sums it to zero. The blind space has been measured at exactly 104
dimensions, and the preceding cycle identified those 104 dimensions with the
span of the differences of the 192 eight-piece exact covers.

Every one of those statements is a statement over the rational numbers. The
pieces, though, are things, and a labelling of them by whole numbers is the
natural object; a rational span says nothing about which whole-number
labellings are reachable. This cycle asks the whole-number question and
answers two halves of it.

**First half, and the result.** Among the 192 exact covers, two of them can
share at most 4 pieces. A pair that shares 4 differs by exchanging 4 pieces
for 4 others, so its difference vector is supported on 8 pieces with entries
-1 0 1, and there are 384 such pairs. These are the smallest differences the
covers admit. The finding is that these 384 smallest exchanges do not merely
span the blind space over the rationals: **they generate the blind lattice
over the whole numbers.** Every whole-number labelling of the pieces that all
15800 cuttings sum to zero is a whole-number combination of four-for-four
exchanges. What the cuttings cannot see is generated by the smallest thing
they cannot see. The same holds already for the 191 differences taken from a
single fixed cover.

**Second half, and the counterweight.** The lattice the cuttings measure and
the lattice they cannot see are orthogonal complements over the rationals, so
one might expect a whole-number labelling to split into a measured part and a
blind part, each with whole-number entries. It does not. The two lattices
glue inside the whole-number labellings at index 2^114 x 3 x 5^30, and the
finite group that index counts has 42 cyclic parts, of orders 2, 10, 20, 40,
80, 160, 320 and 960 with multiplicities 12, 8, 2, 2, 8, 8, 1 and 1. The
smallest whole number carrying every labelling into an integral measured part
plus an integral blind part is 960.

Alongside these, the same technique settles a question left standing by two
earlier cycles: whether the measured ranks 88 and 105 are features of
characteristic zero. They are not. Both hold over every prime field.

## The smallest exchange, and why it is smallest

Write M for the cover-by-piece table: 192 rows, one per exact cover, 192
columns, one per piece, a one where the cover uses the piece. It is 8-regular
on both sides (C2). Off the diagonal, two covers share 0, 1, 2 or 4 pieces,
with counts 30144, 3840, 1920 and 768, so each cover shares 0 pieces with 157
others, 1 piece with 20, 2 pieces with 10, and 4 pieces with 4; those add to
191, which is all the other covers (C3).

Two covers that share s pieces have difference vector supported on exactly
2(8 - s) pieces, since each uses 8 pieces and they agree on s of them. The
support therefore shrinks as the sharing grows, and the largest sharing gives
the smallest difference. The largest sharing is 4, so the smallest difference
has support 8, entries -1 0 1, and there are 384 of them (C4).

Each is blind: a cutting meets every cover in exactly one piece, so it sums
any difference of two covers to zero. The runner checks this directly rather
than by the argument, forming the 15800 by 384 image and gating that its
largest absolute value is 0 (C5).

## One minor of determinant one, and what it buys

For an integer matrix of rank r, the greatest common divisor of all r by r
minors is the product of the invariant factors of its row lattice. A single r
by r minor of absolute value 1 therefore forces that divisor to be 1, hence
every invariant factor to be 1. This is a witness, not a search: one minor
settles a quantity defined over all of them, and no enumeration is involved.

Three consequences follow at once, and all three are used below.

- The rank is r over **every** field, not only over the rationals. Reduction
  of a determinant-one minor stays invertible at every prime, and rank over a
  prime field never exceeds rank over the rationals.
- The quotient of the whole-number labellings by the row lattice is free, of
  rank the number of columns less r. There is no torsion anywhere.
- The row lattice is **saturated**: it is exactly the set of whole-number
  vectors in its own rational span, with nothing missing.

The last one is the lever. Two saturated lattices of the same rank, one
contained in the other, are equal — there is no room between them. So to
show that a set of blind vectors generates the whole blind lattice it is
enough to show that its rational span has the full blind dimension and that
its own row lattice is saturated, and the second of those is a single
determinant.

The runner does not take the witness on trust. It computes, at each rank, two
different minors chosen by walking the columns in opposite orders, and gates
that both have absolute value 1 and that their greatest common divisor is 1.
It also runs the witness against fixed controls: determinants of 0, 144 and 1
computed by the same routine, and the witness applied to a diagonal whose
invariant factors are known by hand not to be all one, where it correctly
returns 144 from minors 144 and 144 (C11). A test that could only ever say
"one" would carry no information; this one says other things when other
things are true.

## The blind lattice is generated over the whole numbers

The three exact rational ranks are measured together, each as the exact rank
of a square product with a transpose, which is legitimate because a matrix
and that product have the same kernel over the rationals: the cutting table
has rank 88, the cover table 105, and the 384 smallest exchanges 104. Since
88 plus 104 is 192, the smallest exchanges span the whole blind space over
the rationals (C6).

The 384 smallest exchanges then have a 104 by 104 minor of absolute value 1,
from each of two opposite column orders, with greatest common divisor 1. Their
row lattice is saturated. It sits inside the blind lattice, which is saturated
because it is a kernel, and both have rank 104. They are therefore equal
(C9). **104 four-for-four exchanges are a whole-number basis of the blind
lattice**, and every whole-number blind labelling is a whole-number
combination of them.

The same statement survives a much stronger restriction. Fix one cover and
take only its 191 differences to the others — a set that includes exchanges
of every size, not just the smallest, but that is anchored at a single point.
Its rank is again 104 and it again carries a minor of absolute value 1 (C10).
One cover with its differences to the rest already generates the whole blind
lattice.

## The two ranks hold in every characteristic

The witness applies to the two tables themselves. The cutting table has an 88
by 88 minor of absolute value 1 (C7); the cover table has a 105 by 105 minor
of absolute value 1 (C8). So the cutting table has rank 88 and the cover table
rank 105 over every field, and the quotients of the whole-number labellings
by their row lattices are free, of ranks 104 and 87.

This is checked directly as well as derived. Over thirteen prime fields the
cutting table holds rank 88 and the cover table 105, a second and independent
scalar routine reproduces 105, and the fixed control — a diagonal whose rank
genuinely drops at the smallest primes — falls to 0, 1 and 3 (C12). The
control's expected values are not written into the runner as
constants; they are computed from the arithmetic of the diagonal itself, so
the gate would fail if either the routine or the arithmetic were wrong.

The 104 blind directions are thus a fact about the object over the whole
numbers and over every prime field alike, not an artefact of working over the
rationals.

## The counterweight: the two lattices do not glue

Write L for the lattice the cuttings measure — the row lattice of the cutting
table, rank 88 — and K for the blind lattice, rank 104. Over the rationals
they are orthogonal complements, and 88 plus 104 is 192, so together they span
every rational labelling. Both are saturated. It is tempting to conclude that
every whole-number labelling splits into a whole-number measured part plus a
whole-number blind part.

It does not. Stacking a basis of L on a basis of K gives a 192 by 192 integer
matrix whose determinant is the index at which the two glue inside the
whole-number labellings, and that index is

    2^114 x 3 x 5^30

which is not 1 (C13). The runner establishes this three ways that must agree
— the determinant of the stacked basis, and the two inner-product
determinants of L and of K separately — and then repeats the whole
computation from a second, independently chosen pair of bases, obtained by
walking the rows in reverse. All six numbers agree. A basis-dependent answer
would have been a defect rather than an invariant, and the check is not
vacuous: the two inner-product determinants multiply to the square of the
stacked one for any orthogonal pair, so requiring them individually equal is a
real constraint. The control is a pair of lattices in the integer plane whose
gluing index is 2 by hand, and the routine returns 2 from all three routes
(C17).

## The group behind the index

An index is a number; the group it counts is the invariant. The quotient of
the whole-number labellings by the two lattices together is a finite abelian
group, and it is measured here from three matrices of three different sizes:
the 88 by 88 inner-product matrix of L, the 104 by 104 inner-product matrix of
K, and the 192 by 192 stacked basis. Classically the first two must agree,
because orthogonal complements inside the whole-number labellings have
isomorphic discriminant groups, and the third is the quotient by definition.
All three return the same group (C14). Three different sizes, one answer.

The group has 42 cyclic parts:

| order | 2 | 10 | 20 | 40 | 80 | 160 | 320 | 960 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| how many | 12 | 8 | 2 | 2 | 8 | 8 | 1 | 1 |

Their orders divide one another in turn, so the largest is the exponent of the
group, and their product is exactly the index measured independently in the
previous section — a tie the runner gates rather than leaves to inspection
(C15). The exponent is 960, which is 5 times the 192 pieces. It is the
smallest whole number n such that n times any labelling of the pieces splits
into a whole-number measured part plus a whole-number blind part. Every
denominator this object needs is a divisor of 960, and no smaller number
suffices.

Two smaller observations sit inside the table. The whole index carries a
single factor of 3, and it is isolated in the one part of order 960; every
other part is built from 2 and 5 alone. And the parts of order 10, 80 and 160
each occur 8 times, matching the 8 pieces in a cover and the 8-regularity of
the cover table, though nothing here shows that match is more than a
coincidence of counts.

The invariant-factor routine is controlled the same way as the determinant
witness. It is handed four diagonals whose answers are known by hand: it
returns 2 and 2 6 12 unchanged, and it returns 1 6 and 2 12 from the diagonals
2 3 and 4 6, which are *not* their own entries (C16). A routine that simply
read off a diagonal would fail on the last two, so a report of all ones is
information.

## Boundary

- **Support 8 is the smallest support among differences of covers, not among
  blind vectors.** The derivation shows only that a pair sharing s pieces
  differs on 2(8 - s) pieces and that s is at most 4. Whether some blind
  vector has support below 8, or whether every blind vector of support 8 is a
  four-for-four exchange, is untouched here.
- **The generation result is about the lattice, not about positivity or
  size.** That every blind labelling is a whole-number combination of
  four-for-four exchanges says nothing about how many exchanges are needed,
  how large the coefficients are, or whether a combination with small
  coefficients exists.
- **The exponent's relation to the piece count is observed, not derived.**
  960 is 5 times 192 as measured. Nothing here forces that relation, and it
  should not be read as a structural identity until something derives it.
- **The eight-fold multiplicities in the group are likewise observed.** They
  match the 8 pieces in a cover numerically; no argument connects them.
- **The gluing index is measured, not derived.** The three-way agreement and
  the two independent bases establish that it is an invariant of the pair of
  lattices. Why that invariant is 2^114 x 3 x 5^30 rather than something else
  is open, and this cycle offers no account of it.
- **The witness is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one.** A minor of
  absolute value 1 proves saturation; failing to find one would prove nothing.
  Every witness reported here succeeded, so the asymmetry does not bite, but
  it bounds what the technique could ever be used to deny.
- Nothing here bears on any physical claim. The object is a finite
  combinatorial one and every statement about it is a count.

## Next

The whole-number picture of the blind space is now complete enough to be used
rather than extended, and it points two ways.

The first is at the group. 42 cyclic parts is a small enough number that a
decomposition of the 192-dimensional cover space ought to account for them
part by part, and the parts already carry visible structure: a divisibility
chain, one isolated factor of 3, three multiplicities of 8. A decomposition
that named the 42 would very likely also name the 87-fold kernel that the
preceding cycle left as the whole of the 104 — the two are questions about
the same table asked over different rings.

The second is at what the cuttings do see. The measured lattice has rank 88
inside 192 columns, and the natural question is how that 88 is distributed
across the pieces: whether the cuttings favour some pieces over others, or
see every piece to precisely the same degree. That is a single projection
away and is the obvious next measurement.

Standing from earlier cycles, and untouched here: the multiplicities of the
whole-number eigenvalues of the sharing table, the identity of the eigenvalues
that are not rational, whether the fewest-part closed refinement of the
sharing classes carries the 104-dimensional span, and whether the
combinatorial symmetry group of the sharing structure exceeds the cube
symmetries.
