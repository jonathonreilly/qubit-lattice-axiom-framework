## 3. Threshold classification, including degeneracies and a nonzero witness

A(p,p)=0. If A is not identically zero, its Taylor expansion at (0,0) has a
first nonzero homogeneous polynomial A_n of a finite degree n>=1. To justify
finiteness, suppose all coefficients vanish. Real analyticity then gives
A=0 in a neighborhood. If u is nonzero at any point of that neighborhood,
z is a constant complex multiple of u there, hence on all R by the real
analytic identity principle. If u vanishes on the whole neighborhood it
vanishes everywhere. In either case A vanishes on R2. This contradicts the
assumed nonzero pair. No assertion of a nonzero coefficient for every input
is being substituted for this argument.

The first homogeneous term of G is ell^(n+1) A_n(x,y). In the angular
integral, every homogeneous term of odd total degree integrates to zero
under (x,y)->(-x,-y). Taylor expansion in (14) therefore proves

    rho(2m+s)=c_n s^n+O(s^(n+1)),
    c_n=(ell^(2n+2)/4) integral_0^(2pi)
                       |A_n(cos phi,sin phi)|^2 dphi>0. (15)

A nonzero homogeneous polynomial cannot vanish on the entire unit circle,
which proves strict positivity. The error is an ordinary fixed-input
asymptotic. In fact rho(2m+s) has a full integer-power expansion at zero,
and is C-infinity on the closed half-line s>=0, with all derivatives rapidly
decaying at infinity. Near zero this follows from the convergent real
analytic angular series; away from zero it follows by differentiating (14)
and using the Schwartz estimates on G. This also supplies the derivative
control needed in Section 4.

The degree-one coefficient is particularly simple. Put

    d=u(0)z'(0)-z(0)u'(0).

Then A_1(p,q)=d(q-p). If d!=0, n=1, and since
integral_0^(2pi)(sin phi-cos phi)^2 dphi=2pi,

    rho(2m+s)=c_1 s+O(s^2),
    c_1=(2pi m^2/v^4)|d|^2.                            (16)

If d=0, the coefficient in (16) is zero. Either A=0, or a later integer
order n>=2 in (15) is the leading order. Thus there is no input-independent
nonzero linear onset. For example g=c f with real c has Braw=0 and zero
response at every energy. These zero inputs are retained, not discarded by
a normalization or genericity convention.

There are allowed fixed inputs with a nonzero linear onset. Take a nonzero
real even compact smooth scalar bump phi with nonzero integral and set

    f=(phi,0)^T,   g=(0,phi)^T,
    s0=phihat(0)=(2pi)^(-1/2) integral phi(x) dx !=0.

For these inputs,

    u=exp(-i theta/2) phihat,   z=i exp(i theta/2) phihat,
    u(0)=s0, z(0)=i s0,
    u'(0)=-i(v/(2m))s0,   z'(0)=-(v/(2m))s0,
    d=-(v/m)s0^2.

Therefore

    c_1=(2pi/v^2) s0^4
       =[(integral phi)^4]/(2pi v^2)>0.                (17)

No observed value, chosen exponent or positivity target entered this
calculation. The vanishing of A(0,0), and the resulting extra threshold
power compared with two distinguishable scalar particles, comes from the
rank-one positive polarization and antisymmetry. Both momenta are integrated
independently in (10); no sharp total-momentum constraint has been imposed.
It would not be valid to
use a distinguishable-particle phase-space density with a constant matrix
element at the minimum.

A nonzero higher-order case is equally concrete. With the same scalar bump,
take f=(phi,0)^T and g=(-partial_x^2 phi,0)^T. Then z(p)=p^2 u(p), so
A(p,q)=u(p)u(q)(q^2-p^2), and its first nonzero term is
A_2(p,q)=s0^2(q^2-p^2). Consequently n=2 and

    c_2=(pi ell^6/4)s0^4=(2pi m^3/v^6)s0^4>0.

This is a nonzero compact smooth pair with d=0; higher-order degeneracy is
not merely the zero-operator case.

## 4. Controlled long-time connected correlation

Let r(s)=rho(2m+s). The endpoint derivative bounds following (15) permit

A!=0 and real |t| tending to infinity,

    C(t)=exp(-2imt) [n!c_n/(it)^(n+1)
                                  +O(|t|^(-n-2))].    (18)

For d!=0 this reads

    C(t)=-c_1 exp(-2imt)/t^2+O(|t|^-3).                (19)

For A=0 the correlation is zero exactly. The correlation is absolutely
integrable in time for every input in the stated class, but a nonzero
1/t^2 coefficient is conditional on d!=0. Formula (18), rather than a
universal power declaration, covers the degenerate cases.

Here is an alternate check of the first nonzero coefficient. The Fock
inner product of two pairs gives directly

    C(t)=<u,exp(-itE)u><z,exp(-itE)z>
                       -<u,exp(-itE)z><z,exp(-itE)u>. (20)

In the coordinate (12), put b_1(x)=sqrt(k'(x))u(k(x)) and
b_2(x)=sqrt(k'(x))z(k(x)), and S_ij(x)=conj(b_i(x))b_j(x).

    M_ij(t)=exp(-imt) exp(-i pi/4) sqrt(pi/t)
                [S_ij(0)-i S_ij''(0)/(4t)+O(t^-2)].    (21)

One obtains its constants by inserting exp(-epsilon x^2), evaluating the
Gaussian integral and its second derivative, then removing epsilon;
a smooth cutoff near zero and integration by parts away from zero bound

stationary-phase fit. Now S(0) has rank at most one, and direct expansion
of the two-by-two determinant gives

    tr(adj(S(0)) S''(0))
       =2|b_1(0)b_2'(0)-b_2(0)b_1'(0)|^2

Terms involving b'' and b vanish in this trace because adj(S(0)) annihilates
the rank-one vector. The determinant of (21) is consequently
-c_1 exp(-2imt)/t^2+O(t^-3), in agreement with (19). In particular the
putative 1/t term from multiplying two one-particle leading terms cancels.
