## 3. Why the integration-by-parts theorem permits the weights

For the localized term put `H_j(k)=f(k)C_j(k)`, as a vector in the full
clock/source/emitter Hilbert space. The inherited regularity makes it
absolutely continuous with zero endpoint trace. Its derivative is
`H_j'=f'C_j+f C_j'`. Integration by parts gives

    ||integral H_j exp(-iEt) dk||
      <=(1/|t|) integral [||H_j'||/E'
                                +|E''| ||H_j||/(E')^2] dk
      <=(1/(v|t|)) integral [|f'| ||C_j||+|f| ||C_j'||
                                  +(E2/v)|f| ||C_j||] dk.

Division by `sqrt(2pi)` gives the proposed weighted `D_j` estimate. The
old theorem's separate suprema are sufficient bounds for this integral,
not a required extra step in the identity. The replacement is therefore
a valid sharpening of that proof. The inherited uniform regularity is
still available for constructing and normalizing the exact scattering
isometry; it has not been discarded merely because a smaller integral is
now estimated.

For an unwanted incoming coordinate, the phase derivative has magnitude
at least `n+vt`. After integration by parts, the amplitude is bounded by
`A0/[sqrt(2pi)(n+vt)]`, where
`A0=integral |f'|+(E2/v)F0`. Summing the squares using
`sum_(n>=0)(n+vt)^-2<=2/(vt)` for `vt>=1` gives
`A0/sqrt(pi vt)`. For the negative-site part of the outgoing wave, apply
